## 1. The trade and its reader

Two different customers read an exterior cleaning site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The homeowner** has a drive gone green and slippery, render streaked with black, a roof furred with moss, or a patio covered in dark spots that won't scrub off. Many have already tried a hired pressure washer and made it worse: stripes on the patio, sand blasted out of the joints. Their fear is paying someone to do the same damage with a bigger machine. Copy for them explains what the growth or stain actually is, and why the method has to match the surface.
- **The commercial client** (a site manager, landlord or managing agent) needs car parks, cladding and walkways clean and safe, with little disruption. Their fear is slips, complaints, wash water in the wrong drain and a contractor who can't work around opening hours. Copy for them is short and practical: surfaces, access, runoff, timing.

The voice is an operator who reads a surface before touching it, explaining plainly why the wall gets a low pressure wash and the drive a rotary clean. Never a before and after showreel.

**Why type A, not B.** Exterior cleaning repeats, but it isn't a round. Most jobs are quoted per surface, booked once and done in a day, and the customer comes back when the growth returns, often a year or more later. The template's A steps (quote, schedule, job) and its "signs you need it" cards fit how people buy this work. B's steps (book, first visit, round) would promise a visiting rhythm most clients don't run. If a client sells planned maintenance contracts to commercial sites, that service reads well as schedule led inside A.

**The split inside type A.**

- **Problem led** (write symptom, cause, consequence): Soft washing, Roof cleaning, Render cleaning, Cladding cleaning, Driveway and patio cleaning, Brick and stone cleaning, Decking cleaning, Oil and rust stain removal, Graffiti removal, Fascia and soffit cleaning, Conservatory cleaning. This is most of the trade.
- **Schedule led** (write what slips and when): Block paving and concrete sealing, Solar panel cleaning, and commercial cleaning where a client confirms planned visits. Sealing has a life; a seal past it lets weeds and stains back in.
- **Desire led** is rare here. Cleaning before selling a house or before an event is the only real case, and it is written as the situation, not a symptom.

**Seasonal niche.** Exterior cleaning is seasonal. Where code swaps a block 4 card for the time of year, write the reason in the weather or the growth: biocides need a dry spell to work, sealers need a dry surface and mild temperatures, algae and moss grow back fastest through damp autumns and winters, and water left on paving before a frost makes it slippery. Never "spring is a busy time".

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Growth follows shade and damp, and the surfaces it grows on follow the housing.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): tree cover and shaded, north facing elevations; damp valleys, river edges and coastal air; housing era and what it was usually built with (block paved drives on 1990s and 2000s estates, rendered and cladded new builds, older brick terraces with soft facing brick and lime mortar, local stone where the research confirms it); conservation areas and listed buildings where the research confirms them; drainage that runs to rivers or streams, which makes runoff control matter more.

**Every anchor must change the work.** Tree cover is only worth mentioning if it then explains why one side of a house greens over faster. Soft Victorian brick is only worth mentioning if it then explains why high pressure would erode it. A river at the bottom of the street is only worth mentioning if it then explains why wash water is kept out of the surface drains.

**Do not:** claim drives, roofs or walls in an area are filthy or neglected; turn an era into a claim about today's houses ("roofs here are covered in moss"); invent slip or accident figures.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **The surface decides the method.** Hard concrete can take a hot, high pressure rotary clean. Render, cladding, roofs, soft brick and timber cannot. Name the surface and the method together, every time, and the reader sees the knowledge they are paying for.
2. **Soft washing kills; pressure only moves.** Pressure takes off what it hits and leaves roots in the pores, so green comes back in months. A soft wash applies a biocide at low pressure, kills the growth, and lets it rinse or weather away. Black lichen spots on patios fade over weeks after treatment rather than blasting off. Say so honestly.
3. **Name the damage the wrong pressure does.** Furred decking, stripes in concrete, joints emptied of sand, scarred render that now shows a patch, water driven under roof tiles, cracked or broken tiles from being walked on, eroded brick faces and raked out mortar. These are the customer's real fears.
4. **Finish the job the clean starts.** Block paving cleaned without the joints refilled with fresh kiln dried sand lets weeds and movement back in. A sealer applied before the surface is dry clouds. The work after the clean is often what decides how long it lasts.
5. **Don't overpromise permanence.** Oil soaks into concrete and may lighten rather than vanish; rust can leave a shadow; growth returns on shaded surfaces. Say what the clean does and what slows the return.
6. **Account for the water.** Wash water and biocide runoff should not go into surface water drains that run to streams, and plants and ponds near the work need protecting. Write it as the principle of good work, not as this business's process.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type A, Soft washing): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Soft washing | card_text: Render that went green within months of being pressure washed still had live growth in its pores. Our [soft washing](/services/soft-washing/) uses low pressure and a biocide that kills algae, moss and lichen at the root, so the surface is cleaned without being blasted and the growth is slower to return.
```

Why it works: opens on the result a customer has seen after the wrong method, gives the reason, and names the method without overpromising.

### Category page service card (type A, Driveway and patio cleaning): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Driveway and patio cleaning | card_text: Block paving gone green and slippery usually needs more than a wash. Our [driveway and patio cleaning](/services/driveway-and-patio-cleaning/) lifts the growth from the surface and the joints, and a good job finishes by brushing in fresh kiln dried sand, because joints left empty let weeds back and the blocks start to shift.
```

Why it works: names the surface and the step after the clean that cheap work skips, written as the principle of a good job rather than this business's fixed process.

### Block 4 card, problem led (Soft washing): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Black spots that won't scrub off | card_body: Those dark spots on a patio or wall are usually lichen, which grips into the surface rather than sitting on it. Pressure washing hard enough to shift them usually scars the stone first. A biocide treatment kills the lichen, and the spots then fade over the following weeks as the weather breaks them down.
```

Why it works: corrects the common belief, gives the cause, and is honest that the result takes weeks, not one visit.

### Block 4 card, damage from the wrong pressure (Roof cleaning): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Moss on the roof | card_body: Moss on a roof is mostly a gutter problem, as clumps wash down and block the outlets. Blasting it off at high pressure can strip the tile surface and drive water up under the laps, and walking on tiles can crack them. Careful removal, then a biocide to slow regrowth, avoids both.
```

Why it works: keeps the threat in proportion, names the two kinds of damage the wrong method causes, and describes the principle of good work without claiming a process.

### Block 4 card, schedule led (Block paving and concrete sealing): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: A sealed drive that's lost its sheen | card_body: A sealer wears hardest where tyres turn and water sits, and once it thins, oil and growth soak in again and weeds root in the joints. Resealing while the old seal still holds is simpler than stripping a stained drive later, and it needs a dry spell so the sealer cures clear.
```

Why it works: what slips and when, with a reason in the material and the weather, and no fixed interval claimed.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Render cleaning)

```
Render that is pressure washed at the wrong setting can lose its surface, and the damage shows as a patch that never matches the wall again. Water forced into hairline cracks can also work its way behind the render and lift it in a frost. That's why render is cleaned at low pressure with a biocide, and why the type of render is checked first.
```

Why it works: one concrete consequence the customer would see, with its physical cause, written as a reason for the method rather than a scare.

### FAQ answer (Driveway and patio cleaning): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Will pressure washing damage my block paving? | faq_answer: Not if the pressure suits the paving and the joints are refilled. High pressure aimed close to the joints blows the sand out, and paving left like that lets weeds root and the blocks move. A rotary surface cleaner keeps the pressure even, and fresh kiln dried sand brushed in afterwards locks the blocks again. Sealing is optional, and only goes on a dry surface.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, names the real risk, and explains the principle of the method without promising an outcome.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Exterior cleaning in [town] changes from street to street. The tree lined roads near the park keep north facing walls and drives in shade, so algae and moss return there faster than on open estates. The Victorian terraces in the centre were built in soft facing brick with lime mortar, which high pressure erodes, while the newer estates were often finished in render that needs a low pressure soft wash.
```

Why it works: two town facts, each tied to what it means for the method. It describes how the housing was built and where shade falls, not the state of anyone's house.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so a before selling or before an event topic is written as a *decision* instead: which surfaces matter, which method suits each. "Soft washing or pressure washing?" not "Picture your drive gleaming".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: why the drive went green again so fast, what the black spots on the patio are, why the block paving is growing weeds after it was washed, whether moss damages a roof, why render streaks under the windows. Answer with the cause on the surface, then what treats it.
- **Decision posts**: soft washing or pressure washing; seal or don't seal block paving; clean or replace tired paving; clean render or paint it; hire a machine or book a clean. Say which suits which surface, never which wins.
- **Cost guides**: what moves the price. The surface and its area, how much growth or staining, access (rear patios through the house, height on render and roofs), whether joints need refilling, sealing, stain treatment, and runoff control on sensitive sites. Explain why each moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local and seasonal posts**: one shade, damp or housing condition in one named place and what it means for the method at that time of year. Shaded streets and faster regrowth, soft brick and low pressure, a dry spell as the window for biocide and sealer.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: A drive goes green again quickly when pressure washing removed the growth but not its roots. Algae and moss live in the pores of concrete and in the joints, so a jet clears the surface and leaves enough behind to regrow within months. A biocide treatment after cleaning kills what's left and slows the return.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. The cause is physical, and the fix is a method, not a promise.

### Blog section (decision post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: When is soft washing better than pressure washing? | body: Soft washing is better on any surface that high pressure would damage, and pressure washing suits hard, dense surfaces. Render, cladding, roof tiles, painted woodwork, soft brick and stone all fall into the first group, because a strong jet can scar them, strip a coating or force water in behind them.\n\nSoft washing works by chemistry rather than force. A biocide is applied at low pressure, left to kill algae, moss and lichen, then rinsed or left to weather away. Because it kills the growth at the root, it tends to stay clean for longer than a surface that was only blasted.\n\nPressure washing still has its place. Concrete drives, block paving and hard porcelain can take a higher pressure, usually through a rotary cleaner that keeps it even, and many jobs combine both: a pressure clean, then a biocide to slow regrowth. Our [soft washing](/services/soft-washing/) page explains which surfaces it covers.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact the trade facts rows would supply (which surfaces need low pressure, how biocide works, where pressure still fits), and it presents both methods honestly. The link sits in the closing paragraph.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** DIY guides for roof cleaning or anything at height; chemical mixing ratios or instructions for using biocides; posts that treat a pressure washer setting as the whole answer; before and after galleries in words with no surface or method named; "it depends" without naming the surface.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any cleaning site in the country, with no surface, growth or method anywhere in the paragraph.
- Promises that growth won't return, oil will vanish or a drive will look new.
- Ignoring the damage the wrong pressure does, which is exactly what the customer is worried about.
- Block paving cleaned with no mention of the joints.
- Roof cleaning copy that implies walking the roof is harmless, or treats moss as an emergency.
- Chemical or biocide claims stated as "safe" for pets and plants without the principle of protecting them.
- Claims that houses or drives in an area are dirty or neglected.
- Sealer intervals, warranties or insurance stated as this client's practice when not confirmed.

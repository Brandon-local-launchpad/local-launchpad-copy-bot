## 1. The trade and its reader

Three different customers read a driveway and paving site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The new driveway customer** wants somewhere to park off the street. They are spending a large sum once, on the most visible part of the property, and their fear is a drive that ruts, cracks or holds a puddle at the door within two winters, with the doorstep trader and his leftover tarmac behind that. Copy for them talks about what is underneath (the dig, the base, the edging, the fall) and where the rain goes, because that is where cheap work cuts corners.
- **The repair customer** has a drive that has sunk, rutted or cracked, and wants to know whether it can be put right or has to come up. Copy for them names the cause and says plainly when a patch will hold.
- **The commercial client** (car parks, line marking) cares about the site staying open while the work is phased. Write for them only where the client confirms commercial work.

Two rules catch customers out more than anything else here: front garden permeability and dropped kerb consent. Copy that explains both plainly, as what usually applies, earns more trust than any claim about finish.

The voice is a groundworker who knows ground, water and weight, explaining it plainly to a homeowner. Never a showroom selling kerb appeal.

## 2. How this trade's services sort into the template

Code chooses the business type and the block briefs. This section tells you what each block should be *about* for each part of this trade, so the copy fits the brief.

| Category (GBP) | Type | Block 4 is about |
|---|---|---|
| Paving Contractor | A project | A mix: symptoms of a failed base for repairs and relays (ruts, sinking, rocking, puddles, weeds in the joints); situations for new driveways, patios and paths |
| Concrete Contractor | A project | Cracking, a flaking surface, worn or faded sealer on imprinted concrete; for new concrete, the situation that leads to it |
| Road Construction Company | A project (commercial only) | Lines that have faded, a layout that no longer works, potholes and standing water across a car park |

The Paving Contractor row matches the Landscaper pack's Paving row: the base is the subject, and the visible fault is read as a sign of what is happening below it.

**Desire led, problem led and schedule led, inside type A.** Nobody has symptoms of wanting a new driveway, so for desire led services the "sign" is the customer's situation, not a fault:

- **Desire led** (write the situation): block paving, resin bound driveways, gravel driveways, tarmac surfacing, concrete driveways, imprinted concrete, tar and chip surfacing, permeable paving, dropped kerbs, kerbs and edging, patios, garden paths, garden steps, concrete bases and paths, play area and sports surfacing, bollards.
- **Problem led** (write symptom, cause, consequence): driveway repairs, concrete repairs and resurfacing, retaining walls, car park surfacing where the existing surface has failed.
- **Schedule led** (write what slips and when): driveway sealing, resealing imprinted concrete, car park line marking.

**The permission step.** Two rules the customer often doesn't know about, written as part of the job rather than a hurdle:

- **Front gardens.** Paving a front garden usually needs no planning permission if the surface lets water through, or the rain runs onto a lawn or border within the property. A larger hard surface that sends water onto the road or into the drains usually does need permission. That is why permeable surfaces and falls towards a border or soakaway come up on almost every front garden job.
- **Dropped kerbs.** Crossing the pavement to a driveway usually needs consent from the council that looks after the road, and on a classified road planning permission as well. The council looks at visibility, street trees, lamp posts and whether a car fits without overhanging the pavement. Many councils build the crossing themselves or name the contractors allowed to, so never imply the business can promise consent or do the kerb work unless the client confirms it.

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Driveways carry a car's weight on the ground and have to send the rain somewhere, so this trade has strong legitimate local detail. Use it as the research gives it.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): ground type (clay that holds water and moves, chalk or sand that drains freely); slope, especially frontages falling towards the house; housing era and frontage depth (shallow Victorian terrace fronts, interwar semis deep enough to park on, post war estates with shared drives and verges); classified roads; street trees on the pavement line; controlled parking zones and surface water flood risk where the research confirms them; conservation areas with front garden controls.

**Every anchor must change the work.** Clay is only worth mentioning if it then explains a deeper dig or a stronger base. A slope towards the house is only worth mentioning if it then explains a channel drain across the front. A tree lined street is only worth mentioning if it then explains why a dropped kerb position may be limited.

**Do not:** claim drives in an area are cracked, weedy or sinking; claim a named council's process, fees or timescales unless the research gives them; name the council's approved kerb contractors; turn an era into a judgement about today's frontages.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **Name the layer.** "A dig to firm ground, a compacted stone base, a sand bed and edging set in concrete", not "a quality installation". Every surface is the top of a stack, and naming the stack separates good copy from a brochure.
2. **Put every failure in the base or the water.** Ruts mean a base too thin for the weight. Blocks spreading at the edge mean the edging gave way. Puddles mean the fall was never there. Potholes start where water got into tarmac and froze. Name the reason and the copy reads as knowledge rather than warning.
3. **Water decides the design.** Every drive sends rain somewhere: through the surface, along a fall to a border, or into a channel drain and soakaway. Say where it goes. The front garden rule lives here too.
4. **Write the rules as the customer meets them.** Permeability and dropped kerb consent are written as what usually applies, with the practical moment they bite ("worth settling before the design", "worth applying before the drive is built"). Never name a regulation, and never promise an outcome only the council controls.
5. **Repair or relay is a diagnosis, not a sale.** Sunken blocks over a sound base can often be lifted and relaid; a base that has failed across the drive can't be patched for long, and on resin, tarmac or concrete a repair usually shows.
6. **Weather sets the laying day.** Resin and tarmac need dry conditions, concrete needs protecting from frost while it cures, and a new surface needs time before a car goes on it. Describe the principle; never state this business's timings.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type A, Paving Contractor): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Driveways | card_text: A drive that ruts or sinks has usually failed underneath, not on the surface. Our [driveways](/driveways/) work starts with the dig and a compacted stone base sized for a car's weight, then firm edging so nothing spreads, and a fall that sends rain to a border or drain rather than the road.
```

Why it works: opens on the fault the reader fears, names the hidden layers in plain words, keeps the link inside a sentence, and carries no local detail (cards don't). It describes the principle of a sound drive rather than claiming a fixed process.

### Category page service card (type A, Block paving): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Block paving | card_text: Block paving that dips in the tyre tracks or spreads at the edges has usually moved in the base, not the blocks. Good [block paving](/services/block-paving/) sits on a compacted stone base and a screeded sand bed, held by edging set in concrete, with kiln dried sand brushed into the joints.
```

Why it works: the opening symptom leads straight to the hidden layer, matching the Landscaper pack's patio card. The methods named are standard methods from the trade facts, not products, and they are written as what good work involves.

### Block 4 card, problem led (Paving Contractor): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Water pooling by the door | card_body: A puddle that sits against the house after rain usually means the drive was laid without enough fall, or has sunk towards the wall since. Water held there soaks into the base and softens it, and against the house it can reach the brickwork. The fix depends on whether the base is sound enough to relay on.
```

Why it works: symptom, cause, then what it leads to, without a scare. It is honest that the answer depends on the base, which is the repair customer's real question.

### Block 4 card, desire led (Paving Contractor): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Front lawn you'd rather park on | card_body: If the car lives on the street while the front garden sits unused, a driveway is the usual answer. The surface matters as much as the look: gravel, resin bound or permeable block paving lets rain through, which in most cases means a front garden can be paved without planning permission.
```

Why it works: there is no fault to describe, so the "sign" is a situation the reader recognises. The front garden rule arrives as useful information, written as what usually applies.

### Block 4 card, schedule led (Concrete Contractor): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Imprinted concrete gone dull | card_body: The sealer on imprinted concrete wears with traffic and sunlight, and once it thins the colour fades and the surface takes in oil and dirt more easily. Cleaning and resealing while the sealer is worn but not gone keeps the colour, and costs far less than restoring a surface that has been left bare for years.
```

Why it works: what slips, why it slips, and the point at which acting is easier. No interval is stated, because how often is a trade fact that depends on traffic and exposure.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Resin bound driveways)

```
Resin laid over a surface that isn't sound shows every fault beneath it. A crack in old concrete or a soft patch in old tarmac usually comes through the resin within a winter or two, and because the surface is one continuous layer, a single spot can't be lifted and relaid the way blocks can. That's why the existing base is checked first.
```

Why it works: one consequence the customer would actually see, with its cause, written as a reason rather than a scare. It does not imply the reader's current drive is failing.

### FAQ answer, rules question (Dropped kerbs): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Do I need permission for a dropped kerb? | faq_answer: Yes, from the council that looks after the road. Crossing the pavement to a driveway usually needs its consent, and on a classified road planning permission as well. The council checks visibility, street trees, lamp posts and whether the drive is deep enough to park on without overhanging the pavement. It's worth applying before the driveway is built.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, the rule is written as what usually applies with no regulation named, and it closes on the practical moment the rule bites. It does not claim the business handles the application.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Driveway work in [town] depends a lot on the frontage. The Victorian terraces near the centre mostly have fronts too shallow to park on, so the question there is paving that drains rather than parking. The 1930s semis along the main roads have deeper front gardens, but a classified road usually means planning permission alongside dropped kerb consent, so the application comes before the design.
```

Why it works: two town facts, each tied to what it means for the work. It describes the housing and the roads, not the state of anyone's drive, and explains no single service (that is the service pages' job).

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** On the website pages, desire led services are written as the customer's situation. The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog a desire led topic is written as a *decision* instead: what each option suits, and what decides between them. "Resin bound or block paving?" not "Picture your new driveway".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: the faults and rules people search for. Weeds between blocks, ruts, cracked concrete, paving a front garden without permission, getting a dropped kerb. Answer with the cause in the base or the water, or the rule as it usually applies.
- **Decision posts**: resin bound or block paving, tarmac or concrete, gravel or a solid surface, repair or relay. Say which option suits which frontage, slope and use, never which one wins.
- **Cost guides**: the area, how much is dug out and taken away, the base the ground needs, drainage, edging, access for machinery, and the surface chosen. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local and seasonal posts**: one ground, road or weather condition in one named place, and what it means for the work. Clay that moves under a drive, a classified road that changes the kerb application, frost and laying concrete or tarmac in winter.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Front garden paving usually needs no planning permission if the surface lets water through or drains onto a border. A larger hard surface that sends rain onto the road or into the drains usually does need it. Gravel, resin bound and permeable block paving are the common ways to stay within the rule.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. The rule is written as what usually applies, and the close names the practical options rather than a sales pitch.

### Blog section (question post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: Which driveway surfaces let water through? | body: Gravel, resin bound and permeable block paving let water through, and tarmac, concrete and standard block paving usually don't. Gravel drains straight into the stone base below, though on a slope it usually needs a grid to stop it washing down.\n\nResin bound and resin bonded sound alike but work differently. Resin bound mixes the stone into the resin and trowels it smooth, leaving tiny gaps for water to pass through, provided it sits on a base that drains. Resin bonded scatters stone over a solid layer of resin, so the water runs off the top.\n\nPermeable block paving uses blocks with wider joints filled with fine grit, over a deeper base of open stone that holds water and lets it soak away. Standard block paving on sand sheds most rain off the surface.\n\nA solid surface can still meet the front garden rule if it falls towards a lawn, a border or a channel drain feeding a soakaway. Our [permeable paving](/services/permeable-paving/) page explains how each option drains.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact the trade facts rows would supply (how gravel drains, resin bound versus resin bonded, how permeable blocks work, the falls option for solid surfaces), and it never ranks the options. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** generic home improvement content any national site could give (design trends, colour choices); "it depends" without naming the ground, slope or use it depends on; DIY instructions for anything involving machinery, excavation near services or work on the highway; rule posts that name a regulation or state a council's process as fact without research.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any driveway site in the country, with no base, water, ground or frontage detail anywhere in the paragraph.
- Colours, patterns and "kerb appeal" with no mention of the dig, the base or the fall.
- Local detail used as scenery: clay or a main road named with nothing about what it does to the base or the kerb application.
- Telling a front garden customer any surface is fine, or ignoring where the rain goes.
- Implying the business can secure dropped kerb consent, or build the crossing, when the client hasn't confirmed it.
- Symptoms invented for desire led services ("signs you need a resin driveway").
- Patching sold as a lasting fix for a base that has failed across the drive.
- Promises about how long a surface lasts, or that weeds will never come through.
- Guarantees, free quotes, laying times or "ready to drive on" timings stated as this client's practice when not confirmed.

## Notes for Brandon

- Business type: all three categories are type A. Paving Contractor block 4 is a mix (failed base symptoms plus new driveway situations), worded to match the Landscaper pack's Paving row so a site carrying both niches says the same thing about bases.
- Driveway sealing and resealing are schedule led inside type A rather than type B, because customers book them occasionally, not on a round.
- The front garden rule is stated without the area threshold; the trade facts pack supplies it. Dropped kerb guidance never says who builds the crossing, because councils differ.
- Road Construction Company is treated as commercial only, per the inputs. If a domestic client chooses it as a secondary category, the block 4 guidance will read wrongly and code may need to drop it.

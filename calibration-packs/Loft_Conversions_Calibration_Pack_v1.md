How good copy reads in this trade. Copybot loads this pack on every page type alongside the CORE prompt, the niche entry and (on service and category pages) the trade facts pack.

What lives where, so nothing is said twice:
- **CORE prompt**: the fields, word counts and every global rule (no dashes, no unconfirmed claims, no placeholders, banned phrases, how to use local research). Not repeated here.
- **Trade facts pack**: what is true of the work (methods, timings, rules, what goes wrong). Not repeated here.
- **Niche entry**: words customers use, words to avoid, fears, step and FAQ briefs. Not repeated here.
- **This pack**: how this trade should sound, how its services sort into the template's blocks, what counts as good local detail for lofts and extensions, and what failure looks like in this trade.

## 1. The trade and its reader

Almost every reader of a loft and extension site is the same customer at different stages: a household that needs more room and has decided, or is deciding, to build rather than move.

- **They want space, not a fix.** A bedroom for a second child, a kitchen big enough to eat in, somewhere for a parent to live close by. Nothing is broken, so the copy never talks about faults.
- **They research for months**, comparing a loft with an extension and checking what the neighbours built. Information beats marketing.
- **Their fears are concrete.** Costs that appear halfway through, an overrun while the family lives in the dust, a loft too low or a stair that eats a bedroom, and work that fails its inspection or causes trouble when the house is sold.

The voice is a builder who has been in hundreds of lofts and knows what a roof will and won't allow, explaining it plainly to a homeowner. Never an estate agent talking about added value.

## 2. How this trade's services sort into the template

Code chooses the business type and the block briefs. This section tells you what each block should be *about* for each part of this trade, so the copy fits the brief.

| Category (GBP) | Type | Block 4 is about |
|---|---|---|
| Home Builder (extensions, shared with Builder) | A project | Situations: a kitchen the family has outgrown, a narrow side return doing nothing, a relative who needs to live close |
| Building Firm (loft and conversion pages, shared with Builder) | A project | Situations, plus what the roof or the space decides: headroom, roof shape, where the stair can go |

**Desire led throughout.** There are no symptoms in this trade, so every block 4 card is the customer's situation. Never invent a fault.

The split that fits this trade is by what the customer wants and what the house allows:

- **More rooms upstairs** (write the situation, then what the roof decides): loft conversions, dormer loft conversions, hip to gable loft conversions, mansard loft conversions, roof light conversions (the Velux page; examples say "roof light conversion"), loft boarding and insulation.
- **More living space on the ground** (write the situation, then what the plot and the planning position decide): house extensions, single storey extensions, double storey extensions, side return extensions, wraparound extensions, conservatories and orangeries, structural alterations and steel beams.
- **Using space already there or apart** (write the situation, then what the existing structure decides): garage conversions, basement conversions, annexes and granny flats, garden rooms and outbuildings.

**Constraint cards.** A good second kind of block 4 card names what decides whether a project works: headroom at the ridge, a trussed roof that needs steels, where the stair lands, a garage floor below the house floor. These are not symptoms; they answer "will it work in my house?"

### Extensions, planning and building regulations

This block is identical in the Builder and Loft Conversions packs. Keep the two in step.

- **Two approvals, never blurred.** Planning permission decides whether a project may be built: size, height and look. Building regulations approval decides whether it is built safely: structure, foundations, fire escape, insulation, drainage and ventilation.
- **Planning.** Many single storey rear extensions, many loft conversions and most garage conversions fall within permitted development and need no application, within limits that depend on the house. Two storey, side and wraparound extensions more often need permission, and any extension to a flat or maisonette does. Conservation areas tighten the rules, and councils can remove permitted development rights for an area or an estate. A lawful development certificate is the usual way to confirm a project is permitted.
- **Building regulations.** Almost every extension, loft conversion and garage conversion needs approval, whether or not it needed planning permission, and building control signs it off at the end. A conservatory separated from the house by external doors, or a small garden room no one sleeps in, is often exempt; knock the conservatory through and it is treated as an extension.
- **Party walls.** Work on a shared wall, or foundations dug close to a neighbour's, usually means serving notice on the neighbours first. It is separate from both approvals.
- **Lofts and annexes.** A rear dormer or a roof light conversion that keeps the roof's shape often falls within permitted development; a front dormer, a mansard and most loft work in a conservation area usually need permission. Every loft conversion needs building regulations approval, including a safe escape route from the new floor. On a semi or terrace the new floor and steels usually bear on the party wall. An annex used as part of the household is usually treated differently in planning from one lived in or let independently, which usually needs permission.
- **Figures come from the trade facts pack.** Examples state the principle and leave depth, height and volume limits to the trade facts rows. Never claim a project will or won't need permission without the site visit.

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Lofts and extensions are shaped by the roofs, the plots and the council. Use them as the research gives them.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): roof shape by era (hipped roofs on 1930s to 1970s semis suit a hip to gable conversion, Victorian terraces with a rear back addition suit an L shaped dormer, houses from the 1970s on often have trussed roofs that need steelwork to open up); plot shape (side returns beside Victorian back additions, integral garages on post war estates, deep gardens on interwar roads); the planning authority, conservation areas and areas where permitted development rights have been removed, only where the research confirms them; ground (clay means deeper extension foundations); terraced streets where scaffold and skips need a licence on the road.

**Every anchor must change the work.** A hipped roof is only worth mentioning if it then explains which loft conversion fits. A conservation area is only worth mentioning if it then explains why a dormer needs an application. Clay is only worth mentioning if it then explains foundation depth.

**Do not:** make local planning claims the research doesn't support (fall back to the general rule and the site visit); claim lofts in an area are tall enough to convert, because headroom varies house by house; claim anything about the inside of homes; promise added value to the house.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **The roof decides the loft.** Every loft card or paragraph names what the roof allows: headroom, a cut roof or trusses, a hip or a gable. That one specific answers the reader's first question.
2. **The stair is part of the design.** Where the new stair lands, and the room it takes from the floor below, decides the layout more than the dormer does. Mention it.
3. **Keep the two approvals apart.** Every mention of planning or building regulations follows the shared block in section 2, with no regulation names. Fire escape from a new floor is the building regulations point customers least expect; name it in plain words.
4. **Write the situation, then the deciding fact.** Desire led cards open on how the household lives now and turn on one fact about the house: the side return width, the garage floor level, the roof shape.
5. **Explain the order of work as a reason.** A loft is usually built from the outside with scaffold, and the opening into the house for the stair comes late; an extension's knockout into the house comes last, once the new structure is weathertight. Write the principle, never this business's timetable.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type A, Loft conversions): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Loft conversions | card_text: Whether a loft will convert usually comes down to three things: headroom under the ridge, how the roof is framed, and where a stair can land without losing a bedroom. Our [loft conversions](/services/loft-conversions/) start by settling those, then the design that suits the roof, from roof lights to a full dormer.
```

Why it works: answers the reader's first question with the real deciding factors, keeps the link inside a sentence, and carries no local detail (cards don't). It describes the principle of good scoping rather than claiming a free survey.

### Category page service card (type A, Hip to gable loft conversions): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Hip to gable loft conversions | card_text: A hipped roof slopes in on the side as well as the front and back, which leaves little standing room in the loft. A [hip to gable loft conversion](/services/hip-to-gable-loft-conversions/) builds the side wall up into a gable, and is often paired with a rear dormer to give full height across the room.
```

Why it works: explains the roof in plain words, then what the work does to it. It names no house type as fact and makes no planning promise.

### Block 4 card, desire led (Home Builder): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: A kitchen the family has outgrown | card_body: If everyone ends up in a kitchen built for two, with the dining table squeezed into the front room, a single storey rear extension is the usual answer. Opening the back wall into one room means a steel beam and building regulations approval, and in many cases no planning application at all.
```

Why it works: a situation the reader recognises, then one specific about the hidden work and the approvals. It is the same card as the Builder pack's, word for word, because the guidance must match.

### Block 4 card, constraint (Building Firm, loft): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: A loft full of timber struts | card_body: Many houses built from the 1970s on have trussed roofs, where diagonal timbers cross the loft and each one holds the roof up. They can still be converted, but the trusses have to be replaced with new floor joists and steel beams first, which is why the roof structure is checked before any design.
```

Why it works: names what the reader can see in their own loft and what it means for the job, without saying no. It sets expectations about structure honestly.

### Block 4 card, desire led (Building Firm, annexes): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: A parent who needs to be close | card_body: When a parent needs support but wants their own front door, an annex in the garden or attached to the house is a common answer. How it will be lived in matters: an annex used as part of the household is usually treated differently in planning from one lived in independently.
```

Why it works: a real situation with no invented symptom, turned on the one planning fact that decides the project, written as what usually applies.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Loft boarding and insulation)

```
Boards laid straight onto the joists squash the insulation beneath them, and squashed insulation loses much of what it does, so the rooms below lose heat through the ceiling. Raising the boards on legs keeps the insulation at its full depth. Boarding gives storage, not a room, though: using a loft as a bedroom needs a full conversion and building regulations approval.
```

Why it works: one consequence with its physical cause, then the honest line between storage and a habitable room, which matches the shared approvals block.

### FAQ answer, rules question (Loft conversions): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Do I need planning permission for a loft conversion? | faq_answer: Often not, if it stays within permitted development. Roof light conversions and rear dormers on houses usually qualify, within volume limits. Front dormers, mansards, flats and most conservation areas usually need an application. Building regulations approval is separate, and every loft conversion needs it, including a safe escape route from the new floor and a stair that meets the rules.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, the rules are written as what usually applies with no regulation named, and it separates the two approvals exactly as the shared block does.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Loft and extension work in [town] follows the shape of the houses. The 1930s semis along the main roads have hipped roofs, so a hip to gable conversion with a rear dormer is the usual way to get full height upstairs. In the Victorian terraces near the station, the narrow side return beside each back addition is where most kitchens grow, and it usually involves the neighbour's wall.
```

Why it works: two town facts, each tied to what it means for the work, including the party wall point from the shared block. It describes the housing, never the inside of anyone's home, and explains no single service.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** On the website pages, every service is written as the customer's situation. The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog every topic is written as a *decision* or a rule instead: what each option suits, and what decides between them. "Dormer or hip to gable?" not "Picture your new bedroom".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: whether a loft is tall enough, planning permission, what building regulations cover in a loft, party wall notices. Answer with the rule as it usually applies, or the deciding fact.
- **Decision posts**: loft or extension, dormer or hip to gable, single or double storey, side return or wraparound, garage conversion or extension. Say which option suits which house and plot, never which one wins.
- **Cost guides**: the type of conversion, the roof structure and steelwork, the stair, foundations, approvals and party wall costs, scaffold and access, and the finish level. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local posts**: one roof type, plot shape or planning condition in one named place, and what it means for building there.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Every loft conversion needs building regulations approval, even when no planning permission is needed. Approval covers the new floor's strength, the stair, insulation and, most of all, a safe way out in a fire. Building control inspects as the work goes on and signs it off, and buyers' solicitors usually ask for that certificate.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. It matches the shared approvals block and names the fire escape point customers least expect.

### Blog section (decision post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: Which loft conversion suits which roof? | body: The roof shape usually decides the type of loft conversion before any other choice is made. A roof light conversion keeps the roof as it is and adds windows in the slope, so it suits lofts that already have good headroom down the middle.\n\nA rear dormer builds a box out from the back slope, giving full height across much of the floor. It is the common choice on terraces and on houses with a gable end, and often falls within permitted development.\n\nA hip to gable conversion suits a hipped roof, where the side slope leaves little usable space. The side wall is built up into a gable, usually with a rear dormer as well.\n\nA mansard replaces the back slope with a steep, almost vertical wall and a flat top. It gives the most room but changes the roof's shape, so it usually needs planning permission. Our [loft conversions](/services/loft-conversions/) page explains what each involves.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact the trade facts rows would supply, and it never ranks the options. The planning points match the shared block. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** design trend and interiors content any national site could give; "adds value to your home" claims; planning posts that state a figure or a council's policy without research; DIY advice on structural work, removing walls or anything near gas or electrics.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any loft or extension site in the country, with no roof, plot, planning or structure detail anywhere in the paragraph.
- Symptoms invented for a desire led trade ("signs your home needs a loft conversion").
- Planning permission and building regulations treated as one thing, or guidance that contradicts the Builder pack.
- "No planning needed" stated as fact without the site visit, or local planning claims the research doesn't support.
- Claims that lofts in an area are tall enough, or that a particular roof will convert.
- Lifestyle and property value language ("dream space", "adds thousands to your home") instead of what is built.
- Ignoring the stair, the fire escape or the party wall, the three things customers learn about too late.
- Fixed prices, programmes, guarantees or "planning handled for you" stated as this client's practice when not confirmed.
- Implying architectural, structural engineering or planning drawing capability the client hasn't confirmed.

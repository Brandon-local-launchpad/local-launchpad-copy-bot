# Builder Calibration Pack v6

How good copy reads in this trade. Copybot loads this pack on every page type alongside the CORE prompt, the niche entry and (on service and category pages) the trade facts pack.

What lives where, so nothing is said twice:
- **CORE prompt**: the fields, word counts and every global rule (no dashes, no unconfirmed claims, no placeholders, banned phrases, how to use local research). Not repeated here.
- **Trade facts pack**: what is true of the work (methods, timings, rules, what goes wrong). Not repeated here.
- **Niche entry**: words customers use, words to avoid, fears, step and FAQ briefs. Not repeated here.
- **This pack**: how this trade should sound, how its services sort into the template's blocks, what counts as good local detail for building work, and what failure looks like in this trade.

The examples use `[CV: key]` tokens and Markdown links exactly as Copybot writes them. Their local detail is illustrative. They show length, angle and depth; never copy them.

## 1. The trade and its reader

Two different customers read a builder's site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The project customer** wants more room or a better house: an extension, a loft, a new kitchen, a garage turned into a room. They research for months before they call, so information beats marketing. Their fears are concrete: costs that appear halfway through, a job that runs months over, the builder who disappears to another site, and work that fails its inspection.
- **The problem customer** has a crack that keeps opening, a damp wall, a sagging lintel or a leak through the ceiling. Copy for them names the cause honestly, says when something is normal, and never sells the biggest fix by default.

Write for commercial clients (fit outs, phasing, keeping a business open) only where the client confirms commercial work.

The voice is a builder who has seen what is behind the plaster, explaining it plainly to a homeowner. Never a developer selling a lifestyle.

**Desire led versus problem led, inside type A.** Nobody has symptoms of wanting an extension, so for desire led services the "sign" is the customer's situation, not a fault:

- **Desire led** (write the situation): house extensions, loft, garage and basement conversions, design and build, garden rooms, conservatories, porch extensions, kitchens, bathrooms, wet rooms, fitted wardrobes, staircases, new floors, decking, patios, disability adaptations, new build homes.
- **Problem led** (write symptom, cause, consequence): structural repairs, foundations and underpinning, damp proofing and waterproofing, property repairs and maintenance, insurance repair work, floor repairs, decking repairs, bath and shower resurfacing.
- **Schedule led** (write what slips and when): repointing within brickwork and masonry, and the maintenance side of property repairs, where a small defect becomes a bigger one if left through a winter.

### Extensions, planning and building regulations

This block is identical in the Builder and Loft Conversions packs. Keep the two in step.

- **Two approvals, never blurred.** Planning permission decides whether a project may be built: size, height and look. Building regulations approval decides whether it is built safely: structure, foundations, fire escape, insulation, drainage and ventilation.
- **Planning.** Many single storey rear extensions, many loft conversions and most garage conversions fall within permitted development and need no application, within limits that depend on the house. Two storey, side and wraparound extensions more often need permission, and any extension to a flat or maisonette does. Conservation areas tighten the rules, and councils can remove permitted development rights for an area or an estate. A lawful development certificate is the usual way to confirm a project is permitted.
- **Building regulations.** Almost every extension, loft conversion and garage conversion needs approval, whether or not it needed planning permission, and building control signs it off at the end. A conservatory separated from the house by external doors, or a small garden room no one sleeps in, is often exempt; knock the conservatory through and it is treated as an extension.
- **Party walls.** Work on a shared wall, or foundations dug close to a neighbour's, usually means serving notice on the neighbours first. It is separate from both approvals.
- **Lofts and annexes.** A rear dormer or a roof light conversion that keeps the roof's shape often falls within permitted development; a front dormer, a mansard and most loft work in a conservation area usually need permission. Every loft conversion needs building regulations approval, including a safe escape route from the new floor. On a semi or terrace the new floor and steels usually bear on the party wall. An annex used as part of the household is usually treated differently in planning from one lived in or let independently, which usually needs permission.
- **Figures come from the trade facts pack.** Examples state the principle and leave depth, height and volume limits to the trade facts rows. Never claim a project will or won't need permission without the site visit.

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Building work is shaped by the council, the housing stock and the ground. Use them as the research gives them.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): the planning authority, conservation areas, removed permitted development rights and green belt edges, only where the research confirms them; housing era and roof shape (hipped roofs on 1930s to 1970s semis suit a hip to gable loft, integral garages on post war estates suit conversion, solid walls and shallow footings on Victorian terraces); ground (clay that shrinks and swells, so foundations go deeper and seasonal hairline cracks are common and usually harmless); access and parking for skips and deliveries on terraced streets.

**Every anchor must change the work.** Clay is only worth mentioning if it then explains foundation depth or which cracks matter. A conservation area is only worth mentioning if it then explains why an extension or dormer needs an application. A hipped roof is only worth mentioning if it then explains which loft conversion fits.

**Do not:** make local planning claims the research doesn't support (fall back to the general rule and the site visit); claim anything about the inside of homes in an area (damp, old wiring, tired kitchens); turn an era into a judgement about today's houses.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **Name the hidden work.** "Trench foundations taken down to firm clay", "a steel beam on padstones", "a damp proof course tied into the existing one". The customer can't see these layers, and naming one is worth more than any reassurance.
2. **The scope is set at the site visit.** The planning position, the ground, the drains and any party wall decide the job and the price. Saying so answers the fear of hidden costs without promising a fixed price.
3. **Explain the order of work as a reason.** The opening into the house usually comes last, once the new structure is weathertight, so the family lives in a closed house for most of the build. Write the principle, never this business's timetable.
4. **Diagnose before prescribing.** Penetrating damp from failed pointing, a bridged damp course or ground raised against the wall is often sold as rising damp, and injecting a chemical course doesn't fix it. Cracks on clay can be seasonal and harmless. Say what is normal, then what isn't.
5. **Keep the two approvals apart.** Every mention of planning or building regulations follows the shared block in section 2, word for word in meaning, with no regulation names.
6. **Information over adjectives.** One clear fact about how the job works beats any claim about how good the work is.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type A, Home Builder): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: House extensions | card_text: Most [house extensions](/services/house-extensions/) start with two questions: what the planning position is, and what is under the ground. We settle both at the site visit, along with where the drains run and whether a neighbour's wall is involved, so the scope and the price are set before anything is built.
```

Why it works: answers the hidden cost fear with how good projects are scoped, names the real unknowns, and keeps the link inside a sentence. It describes the principle of a site visit rather than claiming a free survey.

### Category page service card (type A, Foundations and underpinning): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Foundations and underpinning | card_text: Cracks that run diagonally from the corners of windows and keep widening usually mean a foundation has moved. [Foundations and underpinning](/services/foundations-and-underpinning/) work starts by finding why, whether shrinking clay, a leaking drain or a nearby tree, because underpinning a wall without fixing the cause leaves the ground still moving.
```

Why it works: the symptom leads to the cause, and the cause decides the fix. It is honest that underpinning alone isn't the answer, which is the problem customer's real question.

### Block 4 card, problem led (Construction Company): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Cracks that come and go | card_body: On clay ground, fine cracks that open in a dry summer and close again in winter are usually seasonal movement, not a failing foundation. Cracks wide enough to take a coin, ones that run through the brick rather than the mortar, or doors that start sticking are the ones worth having looked at.
```

Why it works: reassures without dismissing, names what is normal on clay, then gives the signs that matter in plain words. It sells nothing by default.

### Block 4 card, desire led (Home Builder): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: A kitchen the family has outgrown | card_body: If everyone ends up in a kitchen built for two, with the dining table squeezed into the front room, a single storey rear extension is the usual answer. Opening the back wall into one room means a steel beam and building regulations approval, and in many cases no planning application at all.
```

Why it works: a situation the reader recognises instead of an invented symptom, then one specific about the hidden work and the approvals, matching the shared block in section 2.

### Block 4 card, schedule led (General Contractor): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Pointing gone soft and sandy | card_body: Mortar that rubs away under a finger lets rain into the wall, and water that freezes in the joints pushes them open further each winter. On older soft brick, repointing in lime rather than hard cement matters, because cement traps moisture and the brick faces flake off instead of the mortar.
```

Why it works: what slips, why it slips, and why the season matters. The lime point is real trade knowledge that a generic site wouldn't carry.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Damp proofing and waterproofing)

```
A damp patch low on a wall is often blamed on rising damp when the water is coming in from outside: through failed pointing, a leaking gutter, or soil and paving piled above the old damp course. Injecting a new chemical course doesn't stop any of those, so the patch comes back and the replastered wall marks again within a season or two.
```

Why it works: one consequence the customer would actually see, with its cause, and the misdiagnosis that makes it worse. It doesn't claim the reader's house has damp.

### FAQ answer, rules question (House extensions): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Do I need planning permission for an extension? | faq_answer: Often not, for a single storey extension at the back of a house. Many fall within permitted development, within limits on depth and height. Two storey, side and wraparound extensions more often need permission, as does any extension to a flat. Conservation areas tighten the rules. Building regulations approval is separate, and almost every extension needs it either way.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, the rules are written as what usually applies with no regulation named, and it separates the two approvals. The Loft Conversions pack's loft FAQ follows the same shared block.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Building work in [town] starts with the council and the ground. The old centre sits in a conservation area, so rear extensions and dormers there usually go through a planning application. The 1930s semis on the roads out of town have hipped roofs that suit a hip to gable loft, and the clay under them means extension foundations go deeper than on lighter ground.
```

Why it works: three town facts (planning, roof shape, ground), each tied to what it means for the work. It describes the housing, never the inside of anyone's home, and explains no single service.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** On the website pages, desire led services are written as the customer's situation. The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog a desire led topic is written as a *decision* instead: what each option suits, and what decides between them. "Extend or convert the loft?" not "Picture your new kitchen".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: what people search while researching. Planning permission, what building regulations cover, whether a crack is serious, rising damp, party wall notices. Answer with the rule as it usually applies, or the cause.
- **Decision posts**: extend or convert the loft, extend or move, garage conversion or extension, repair or replace. Say which option suits which house and budget, never which one wins.
- **Cost guides**: size and storeys, foundations and the ground, steelwork, drainage, access, approvals, party wall costs and the finish level. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local posts**: one planning, housing or ground condition in one named place, and what it means for building there.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Rising damp is rarer than most damp reports suggest. Many damp patches low on a wall come from water getting in from outside, through failed pointing, leaking gutters, or ground and paving raised above the original damp course. Finding where the water enters comes first, because treating the wrong cause leaves the wall wet.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. It corrects a common belief with physical causes, and sells no treatment.

### Blog section (question post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: What's the difference between planning permission and building regulations? | body: Planning permission decides whether you can build something, and building regulations decide how it must be built. They are separate approvals, handled separately by the council, and one doesn't replace the other.\n\nPlanning looks at the outside: size, height, appearance and the effect on neighbours. Many single storey rear extensions and loft conversions fall within permitted development, so they need no application, though the limits depend on the house and are tighter in conservation areas.\n\nBuilding regulations look at safety and performance: foundations, structure, fire escape, insulation, drainage and ventilation. Almost every extension, loft conversion and garage conversion needs approval, including projects that needed no planning permission. Building control inspects at stages and issues a completion certificate, which buyers' solicitors usually ask for when the house is sold.\n\nA party wall notice is a third step, needed when work affects a shared wall or digs close to a neighbour's foundations. Our [house extensions](/services/house-extensions/) page explains how each fits into a project.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact the trade facts rows would supply (what planning covers, what building regulations cover and the completion certificate, party walls), and it matches the shared block in section 2 exactly. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** generic home improvement content (design trends, colour schemes); planning posts that state a figure or a council's policy without research; "it depends" without naming the house, ground or approval it depends on; DIY advice on structural work, removing walls or anything near gas or electrics.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any builder's site in the country, with no planning, ground, structure or housing detail anywhere in the paragraph.
- Planning permission and building regulations treated as one thing, or "no permission needed" stated without the site visit.
- Local planning claims, conservation areas or removed rights the research doesn't support.
- Claims about the inside of homes in an area.
- Lifestyle language ("your dream home") instead of what is built and what is under it.
- Symptoms invented for desire led services ("signs you need an extension").
- Rising damp or underpinning sold as the default answer to every damp patch or crack.
- Fixed prices, start dates, programmes or guarantees stated as this client's practice when not confirmed.
- Implying design, structural engineering or planning drawing capability the client hasn't confirmed.

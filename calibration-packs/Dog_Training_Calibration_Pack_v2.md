# Dog Trainer Calibration Pack v2

How good copy reads in this trade. Copybot loads this pack on every page type alongside the CORE prompt, the niche entry and (on service and category pages) the trade facts pack.

What lives where, so nothing is said twice:
- **CORE prompt**: the fields, word counts and every global rule (no dashes, no unconfirmed claims, no placeholders, banned phrases, how to use local research). Not repeated here.
- **Trade facts pack**: what is true of the work (methods, timings, rules, what goes wrong). Not repeated here.
- **Niche entry**: words customers use, words to avoid, fears, step and FAQ briefs. Not repeated here.
- **This pack**: how this trade should sound, how its services sort into the template's blocks, what counts as good local detail for dog training, and what failure looks like in this trade.

The examples use `[CV: key]` tokens and Markdown links exactly as Copybot writes them. Their local detail is illustrative. They show length, angle and depth; never copy them.

## 1. The trade and its reader

Three owners read a dog trainer's site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The new puppy owner** wants to get it right from the start: mouthing, settling alone, meeting other dogs calmly. Copy for them is about timing, and what the first months set up for life.
- **The owner with a problem** has a dog that pulls, barks at other dogs, won't come back, or panics when left. They have usually tried something already, and they fear being judged and that the dog is somehow broken. Copy for them names the behaviour in their words, explains why what they tried didn't hold, and never blames them.
- **The owner buying care** (residential training, boarding, day care) fears how the dog is handled when they are not there. Copy for them describes routine and handling, not facilities.

The voice is a calm trainer explaining behaviour in the owner's words. Never a drill sergeant, never a dog whisperer.

**Reward based methods only.** Training is described as teaching the dog what to do and making it worth doing: food, play, distance, timing, small steps. Copy never presents corrections, choke or prong collars, shock collars or "dominance" as positive, even in passing. Remote shock collars are banned in England, and copy treats that as settled.

## 2. How this trade's services sort into the template

Code chooses the business type and the block briefs. This section tells you what each block should be *about* for each part of this trade, so the copy fits the brief.

| Category (GBP) | Type | Block 4 is about |
|---|---|---|
| Dog trainer (training services) | C pet service | The behaviours owners describe, what usually sits behind them, and the life stage they belong to |
| Dog trainer: Residential dog training | C pet service | Whether a stay suits this dog and this owner, and how the dog's learning carries back home |
| Dog trainer: Dog boarding, Dog day care | C pet service | The dog's needs during a stay or a day: exercise, rest, company, settling, mixing with other dogs |
| Protection dog training, Family protection dogs (security clients only) | C pet service | Whether a trained dog fits the risk, and what owning or deploying one involves |

**Behaviour led versus care or routine led, inside type C.** Block 4 on the category page is "what we help with", and on a service page "is this right for your dog". The angle depends on the service:

- **Behaviour led** (write the behaviour in the owner's words, what drives it, and why it deepens with practice): Obedience training, Recall training, Loose lead walking, Behaviour modification, Reactive dog training, Nervous and anxious dog training, Aggressive dog rehabilitation.
- **Care or routine led** (write the dog's needs and the routine that meets them): Puppy training, Dog boarding, Dog day care, Residential dog training.
- **Skill led** (write what the dog learns and the owner it suits, never a symptom): Assistance dog training, Scent detection training, Gundog training.

**Life stages and the dog's calendar.** Behaviour has a timeline: the early socialisation window; adolescence, when trained behaviours seem to vanish and that is normal; adulthood, when change works by building a stronger alternative; and later life, when a behaviour change warrants a vet conversation first. Demand has a calendar too: puppies after Christmas and in spring, fireworks around Bonfire Night and New Year, gundog work before the shooting season. Where code swaps a card for the time of year, tie it to what the dog experiences.

**Optional subsection: security and protection dogs (only when the client offers it).** Some trainers also sell protection dogs or site security dog units. When the client confirms this, it is a second market, and the two never blur: owner pages never mention protection work, and protection pages never borrow the owner voice.

- The buyer is a site manager, business owner or household weighing a family protection dog, comparing a dog against cameras and static guards. Sell the capability: a dog detects by scent and sound in the dark and in cover, and deters by presence. Cameras record what happened; a handled dog can stop it happening.
- Say honestly where a dog unit is not the right fit: sites with open public access, risks cameras already cover, households that won't keep up daily handling.
- A family protection dog is first a well trained family dog; control and a calm temperament lead, and the reward based rule still applies.
- A guard dog working on a site usually has to be under a handler's control or secured, with warning notices at the entrances. State it as normal practice.
- No invented crime claims, and licences or accreditations only if confirmed.

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Dogs live where people walk them, so the walk is this trade's richest local detail. Use it as the research gives it.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): walking environments (farmland footpaths, country parks, beaches, towpaths shared with cyclists, busy pavements); livestock near footpaths, where a dog worrying livestock puts the owner at legal risk and the dog at real risk; urban distraction (traffic, crowds, other dogs at close quarters); housing (flats with shared stairwells, terraces without gardens); council dog rules such as seasonal beach restrictions where research confirms them; demographics (young families, working households, retired owners).

**Every anchor must change the work.** Sheep on the footpaths are only worth mentioning if they explain why lead manners come before the lead comes off.

**Do not:** claim dogs in an area are badly behaved or owners irresponsible; invent dog attacks or livestock incidents; name breeds as a local problem.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **The owner's words come first.** "Pulls on the lead", "goes mad at other dogs", "won't come back at the park", "cries when I leave". The trainer term follows only to explain the approach, never to sound clever.
2. **Explain why what they tried didn't hold.** What fails is timing and consistency, not effort: a treat a second late rewards the wrong moment, a gadget lead changes the feel but not what the dog has learned. Saying so takes away the shame.
3. **Every reaction is rehearsal.** Practised behaviour gets stronger, so problems deepen rather than fade. That is the honest reason to start now.
4. **Never promise outcomes or timelines.** No "cured", "fixed", "in six sessions", "guaranteed". Say what the training builds and what the owner practises.
5. **Progress happens between sessions.** A session teaches the owner and sets the next step; the dog changes through daily practice at home.
6. **Methods as principles, and the vet first where it applies.** Distance, reward, small steps, a dog calm enough to learn; no protocols for aggression. Sudden aggression or a change in an older dog can come from pain, so the copy points to a vet check first.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type C, Dog trainer): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Dog trainer | card_text: Pulling on the lead, barking at other dogs and ignoring you at the park are where most owners start. Our [dog trainer](/dog-trainer/) work covers puppy training, recall training and loose lead walking, using rewards and small steps the dog can succeed at, with practice you can keep up between sessions.
```

Why it works: opens on the owner's own words for three behaviours, names confirmed services plainly, keeps the link inside a sentence, and puts progress between sessions rather than promising a result.

### Category page service card (type C, Reactive dog training): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Reactive dog training | card_text: A dog that lunges and barks at other dogs on walks is usually worried, not looking for a fight, and every outburst rehearses the reaction. Our [reactive dog training](/services/reactive-dog-training/) starts at a distance where the dog can still notice another dog and take a reward, then closes the gap slowly.
```

Why it works: reframes the behaviour without blaming the owner, gives the rehearsal reason, and describes the method as a principle (distance and reward) rather than a fixed programme.

### Block 4 card, behaviour led (Dog trainer): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Fine at home, a nightmare on walks | card_body: Plenty of dogs who sit and wait beautifully in the kitchen fall apart outside, and it's rarely defiance. Outside, smells, traffic and other dogs compete with you, and the dog learned the behaviour in a quiet room. Training rebuilds it in stages, from the garden to quiet streets to busier places.
```

Why it works: the owner recognises the line, the cause is in what the dog experiences, and the fix is a principle (building up in stages), not a promise.

### Block 4 card, care or routine led (Dog day care): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Long days home alone | card_body: A young or energetic dog left alone for a full working day often finds its own entertainment, usually chewing, barking or digging. Day care breaks the day up with exercise and company, and good day care builds in quiet time too, because an overtired dog in a busy group tends to get snappier, not calmer.
```

Why it works: names the need in the owner's terms, then shows understanding of the dog with one specific (rest matters as much as play). It describes the principle of good day care without claiming this business's routine.

### Block 4 card, life stage (Puppy training): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: The teenage wobble | card_body: From around six months, many dogs seem to forget everything they knew, recall above all. This adolescent regression is normal and doesn't mean the training has failed. It is the point where keeping up practice matters most, because habits that form during adolescence, good or bad, tend to carry into adulthood.
```

Why it works: reassures without dismissing, names the stage, and gives an honest reason to keep going. No timeline or outcome is promised.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Recall training)

```
Every time a dog runs off and finds something better than coming back, the habit of not coming back gets a little stronger. That's why recall usually gets worse rather than better when it's left, and why a long line on open ground is often used while it's rebuilt: the dog gets room to explore without the chance to practise ignoring you.
```

Why it works: one consequence the owner would actually see, with the rehearsal reason behind it, and a method described as common practice rather than this business's guarantee.

### FAQ answer, rules question (Recall training): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Does my dog have to be on a lead near sheep? | faq_answer: Not always by law on a footpath, but it's the safe choice. A dog that chases or worries livestock can lead to the owner being prosecuted, and in some circumstances a farmer may be entitled to shoot it. Lambing time and fields crossed by public footpaths are where this matters most, so lead manners come before off lead freedom near stock.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, the rule is written as what usually applies with no law named, and it closes on the practical case where it bites. It ties straight back to training.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Dog training in [town] usually means two very different walks. Around the centre, narrow pavements and shared stairwells put dogs nose to nose with other dogs, which is where reactive dogs struggle most and where lead manners get tested every day. Out past the edge of town, the footpaths cross fields with sheep in them, so recall and a solid stop on cue matter before any lead comes off.
```

Why it works: two town facts, each tied to what it means for the training. It describes the walking, not the behaviour of the town's dogs, and explains no single service.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog a behaviour is explained by its cause, and a choice is written as a *decision*: what each option suits, and what decides between them. "Group classes or one to one sessions?" not "Picture your first walk with a calm dog".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: the behaviours owners search for. Why does my dog pull, bark at other dogs, bite as a puppy, not come back, cry when I leave. Answer with what drives the behaviour and why it persists, then the principle that changes it.
- **Decision posts**: group classes or one to one, residential training or sessions at home, harness or collar, a trainer or a vet first. Say which option suits which dog and owner, never which one wins.
- **Cost guides**: what moves the price in this trade. One to one or group, home visits or a venue, how complex the behaviour is (reactivity and aggression need slower work), and the length of a residential stay. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local and seasonal posts**: one walking environment or one point in the dog's calendar in one named place. Preparing a nervous dog before fireworks season, lead manners on footpaths through lambing fields, a busy beach in summer.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Most dogs pull on the lead because pulling works and gets them there faster. Every walk where the lead goes tight and the dog still moves forward rewards the pull again. That's why loose lead walking is taught by making a slack lead the thing that moves the dog forward, in short sessions before long walks.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. The cause is in what the dog learns, and the fix is a principle, not a promise.

### Blog section (decision post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: Does a harness stop a dog pulling? | body: A harness doesn't stop a dog pulling, but it makes pulling safer while the dog learns. A well fitted harness spreads the pressure across the chest instead of the throat, which matters most for small dogs and flat faced breeds that already struggle to breathe. A harness that clips at the chest can also turn the dog slightly when it pulls, which takes some of the force out.\n\nWhat changes the pulling is the training around the harness. The dog learns that a slack lead is what moves it forward, through short sessions in quiet places: stopping when the lead goes tight, moving on when it slackens, and rewarding the dog for walking at your side.\n\nHarnesses that tighten around the chest or legs when the dog pulls work through discomfort, and many dogs simply learn to pull through them. Our [loose lead walking](/services/loose-lead-walking/) page explains how the training is usually built up.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact the trade facts rows would supply (what a harness does to pressure, how slack lead training works, why restrictive harnesses fail), and it steers away from aversive equipment without preaching. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** generic advice any national site could give; dominance or "pack leader" explanations; "every dog is different" without naming what differs; instructions for aggression or biting, where the right answer is what to avoid and to see a qualified trainer and a vet; breed generalisations.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any trainer's site, with no behaviour, life stage or walking detail in the paragraph.
- Trainer jargon opening the sentence ("we use counter conditioning") instead of the owner's words.
- Promises of outcomes or timelines: "cured", "fixed in four weeks", "a calm dog guaranteed".
- Blaming the owner, or implying they caused the problem through what they tried.
- Any aversive method, tool or "dominance" idea presented as positive or as an option.
- The two markets blurred: protection language on owner pages, or a puppy class tone on a security page.
- Protection or residential services implied, or licences stated, without confirmation.
- Local detail used as scenery, with nothing about what it means for recall or lead work.
- Behaviour advice for sudden aggression or an older dog's change with no mention of a vet check.

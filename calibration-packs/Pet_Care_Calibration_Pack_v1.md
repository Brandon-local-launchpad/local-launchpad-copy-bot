## 1. The trade and its reader

Two different customers read a dog walker's or pet sitter's site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The routine customer** works long days and needs the dog walked, the puppy let out or the cat fed while they are out. They are buying the same person turning up at the same time, every week, and noticing when something is off. Their fear is a walker who rushes, loses the dog off lead, or stops coming without warning. Copy for them is calm and practical, and talks about rhythm, what a walk or visit includes, and what gets reported back.
- **The away customer** is going on holiday, into hospital or to a wedding, and needs the animal cared for overnight or for days at a time. Their fear is coming back to an animal that stopped eating, slipped out of a door, or spent the week anxious in a strange place. Copy for them talks about routine kept the same, the questions asked before the first stay, and what happens if the animal seems unwell.

Behind both sits the trust barrier this trade lives on: keys and access to the home, and an animal the owner treats as family. Copy earns that with specifics about how care works, never with claims about loving animals.

The voice is someone who handles dogs and cats every day, explaining plainly what they look for. Never a cute brand voice, and never anyone who claims to fix a dog's behaviour or health. Owners' plain words come first: pulling, barking when left, not eating, hiding.

## 2. How this trade's services sort into the template

Code chooses the business type and the block briefs. This section tells you what each block should be *about* for each part of this trade, so the copy fits the brief.

| Category (GBP) | Type | Block 4 is about |
|---|---|---|
| Dog Walker | C pet service | What we help with: the dog's exercise and company needs, the owner's routine, and the animals that need a different kind of care (puppies, older dogs, nervous dogs, cats and small animals) |

On service pages, block 4 is "is this right for your animal": which dogs or cats a service suits, and which would do better with another option. That honesty is what makes it useful.

**Behaviour led versus care led, inside type C.**

- **Behaviour led** (write the dog's temperament and needs, and which service suits it; never promise a change in behaviour): Solo dog walks, Group dog walks, Secure field walks, Dog day care, Dog home boarding. A dog that pulls toward other dogs, ignores recall near rabbits, or barks when left alone decides between these, and the copy says which suits it.
- **Care led** (write the routine and what a visit or stay covers): Dog sitting, Cat sitting, Cat boarding, Small animal care, Puppy visits, Drop in visits, Pet taxi, Wedding dog chaperone.

Licensing belongs in the copy where it applies. Dog home boarding, dog day care and cat boarding usually need a council animal activity licence in England. Mention a licence as this client's only when confirmed; otherwise state what usually applies.

**Seasonal note.** The trade is steady through the year, with two pressure points worth naming when relevant: school holidays and summer, when boarding and sitting book up, and hot weather, when walks move to early morning and evening because pavements and heat affect dogs. Give the reason in the animal, never "summer is a busy time".

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Good local detail here is the walking environment, never the people or pets in an area.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): the walking environment (named parks, commons, woods, beaches and canal paths from the research); livestock on farmland and footpaths at the edge of town; access land and nature reserves with ground nesting birds, where leads are usually required in spring and early summer; busy roads and unfenced paths that make off lead walking unsafe; beaches with seasonal dog restrictions where the research confirms them; council rules that limit how many dogs one person can walk in some parks, where the research confirms them; housing type (flats without gardens, which push demand for midday walks and puppy visits); commuter patterns, which decide the midday walk.

**Every anchor must change the work.** A common with grazing sheep is only worth mentioning if it explains why dogs go on the lead there. A beach is only worth mentioning if it explains the months dogs can and can't use it. Flats are only worth mentioning if they explain why a puppy needs a midday visit to get outside.

**Do not:** claim dogs or owners in an area have problems (reactive dogs, untrained dogs, lonely pets); invent parks, fields, rules or restrictions the research doesn't give; name dog breeds as typical of an area; use livestock as a scare.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **Name the routine, not the care.** "A 45 minute walk on the lead through the woods, water when you get back, and a note on how they got on", not "pet care to suit you". Specifics about the visit are what make an owner picture handing over a key. Durations only as the client confirms them.
2. **Match the service to the animal, honestly.** Not every dog suits a group walk, and not every cat copes with boarding. Saying which animals a service suits, and which would do better with a solo walk or a home visit, reads as care rather than selling.
3. **Never promise outcomes.** No "a calmer dog", no "cures separation anxiety", no "your pet will love it". Say what the service gives the animal (exercise, company, the same routine) and what it protects against. Behaviour change is a trainer's job and health is a vet's.
4. **Keys and access are the trust barrier.** Answer it with how access and safety work in good practice: the questions asked before the first visit, doors and gates checked, an animal never let off lead until recall has been seen. Key handling, insurance, DBS checks and first aid training only as the client confirms them.
5. **Livestock and leads are local, not decoration.** A dog chasing livestock puts the animal at real risk and the owner at legal risk, and leads are usually required near grazing animals and on some access land in spring. State it plainly as what usually applies, where the walking environment makes it relevant.
6. **Cats and small animals are not small dogs.** Cats usually do better in their own home with visits than in a strange place, and rabbits and guinea pigs need routine and the right temperature as much as food. Write their care in their own terms.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type C, Dog Walker): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Dog Walking | card_text: A dog left from eight until six spends most of the day waiting. Our [dog walker](/dog-walker/) visits break that up with a proper walk at the same time each day, on the lead or off it only once recall has been seen, so the dog gets exercise and company while you work.
```

Why it works: opens on the owner's real reason for searching, names what the walk involves in plain words, keeps the link inside a sentence, and makes no promise about behaviour.

### Category page service card (type C, Dog home boarding): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Dog Home Boarding | card_text: Some dogs struggle in kennels and settle far better in a home. Our [dog home boarding](/services/dog-home-boarding/) keeps your dog's feeding times, walks and bedtime as close to home as possible. In England, boarding dogs in a home usually needs a council animal activity licence, so it's worth asking any sitter to show theirs.
```

Why it works: names the dogs it suits, describes the principle of good boarding (routine kept the same) and states the licensing rule as what usually applies, without claiming this client's licence.

### Block 4 card, behaviour led (Dog Walker, group or solo walks): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Dogs who find other dogs too much | card_body: Not every dog enjoys a group. A dog that pulls toward other dogs, freezes or barks at them usually does better on a solo walk, where the route can avoid busy spots and the pace suits them. It isn't training, but it keeps walks calm while you work on the rest with a trainer.
```

Why it works: names the dog's behaviour in owners' words, matches it to the right service, and is clear about where walking stops and training starts. No promise of change.

### Block 4 card, care led (Dog Walker, cat sitting): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Cats who don't travel well | card_body: Most cats are tied to their territory, and a move to a strange place for a week can put them off food. Cat sitting keeps them at home, with visits for feeding, fresh water, litter trays and time with them, and a note on whether they ate. A cat that stops eating needs a vet's view.
```

Why it works: explains the animal's need in its own terms, describes what a visit covers, and points health concerns to a vet rather than claiming them.

### Block 4 card, needs led (Dog Walker, puppy visits): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Puppies too young to be left all day | card_body: A young puppy usually can't hold on for a full working day, and long gaps make house training harder. Short visits during the day let them out, feed them on schedule and give them company. Until their vaccinations are complete, those visits usually stay in the house and garden rather than out on walks.
```

Why it works: a need every new owner has, its physical reason, and an accurate limit on what the visit includes, stated as what usually applies.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Secure field walks)

```
A dog that bolts after rabbits or deer can cross a road or a field of sheep before a recall lands, and dogs that chase livestock put the animals at risk and the owner at legal risk. That's why some dogs are better exercised off lead in a fully fenced field, where they can run hard without anyone needing to call them back.
```

Why it works: one concrete consequence the owner would recognise, with its cause, written as a reason rather than a scare, and it describes which dogs the service suits.

### FAQ answer, safety question (Group dog walks): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: How many dogs are walked together? | faq_answer: It varies by walker, and some councils set a limit. A number of councils in England cap how many dogs one person can walk in their parks and open spaces, and insurers usually set a limit too. Smaller groups let a walker watch every dog and match dogs that get on, so it's a fair question to ask before the first walk.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, the rule is written as what usually applies without naming a law or a number, and it closes on why the number matters to the dog. (Limits come from the trade facts pack and local research on the day; never from this example.)

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Dog walking in [town] runs between two kinds of ground. On the edge of town, the footpaths cross farmland where sheep graze for much of the year, so dogs stay on the lead there. Closer in, the park and the riverside path take most of the daily walks, and with so many people in flats near the centre, a midday walk is often the only time the dog gets out.
```

Why it works: the walking environment and the housing, each tied to what it means for the walk. It says nothing about the dogs or owners in the town and explains no single service.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** On the website pages, a service is matched to the animal's needs and the owner's routine. The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog those topics are written as *questions and decisions* instead: what decides between a group walk and a solo walk, between boarding and a sitter. "Home boarding or kennels?" not "Imagine Max on holiday".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: what owners search before handing over a key: how long a puppy can be left, whether a walker needs a licence, what to ask before a first stay, what vaccinations boarding usually needs. Answer with what usually applies and the reason in the animal.
- **Decision posts**: group or solo walks, home boarding or kennels, cat sitting or a cattery, day care or a midday walk, a secure field or an open space. Say which option suits which animal, never which is "best".
- **Cost guides**: what moves the price in this trade. Solo or group, walk length, how far the walker travels, how many animals, overnight or drop in, medication or extra care, and bank holidays and peak weeks. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local and seasonal posts**: one walking condition in one named place and what it means for dogs at that time of year. Livestock and lambing near footpaths, leads on access land in spring, beach restrictions, hot pavements, fireworks season.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Dog home boarding in England usually needs a licence from the local council. The licence covers anyone boarding other people's dogs in their home as a business, and councils inspect before granting one. Asking to see it, and checking it covers the number of dogs being boarded, is a reasonable step before a first stay.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. The rule is written as what usually applies, with no regulation named, and it ends on a practical check rather than a claim about this business.

### Blog section (decision post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: Is a group walk or a solo walk better for my dog? | body: It depends on how your dog behaves around other dogs, and the walk should fit the dog rather than the other way round. Sociable, confident dogs often enjoy a small group, because playing and running with others gives them exercise that is hard to match on the lead.\n\nDogs who are nervous, reactive or still young usually do better alone. A solo walk lets the walker choose quieter routes and times, keep the dog on a lead where needed, and avoid situations that build stress, since every bad encounter tends to make the next one harder.\n\nRecall matters too. A dog whose recall isn't solid yet is usually walked on a long line or in a secure field rather than let off with a group in open country, especially near livestock or roads. Many owners use both: a solo walk for some days and a group for others. Our [solo dog walks](/services/solo-dog-walks/) page explains what each walk includes.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact that would come from the trade facts rows (who suits groups, why reactive dogs walk alone, recall and livestock), and it never promises a behavioural outcome. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** training advice or behaviour fixes, which belong to a trainer; health or medication advice, when the honest line is to call the vet; any aversive tool or method presented positively; vaccination schedules or licence rules quoted from memory instead of the trade facts rows; "it depends" without naming what it depends on; cute listicles any national site could give.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any pet care site in the country, with no walk, routine, animal need or place named anywhere in the paragraph.
- Promises about behaviour or health: "a calmer dog", "no more separation anxiety", "happy, healthy pets".
- "Fur babies", "pawsome" and other cute brand voice in place of what the visit actually involves.
- Licences, insurance, DBS checks, pet first aid, GPS tracking, photo updates or maximum group sizes stated as fact when not confirmed.
- Every dog sold a group walk, or every cat sold boarding, with no honesty about which animals a service suits.
- Off lead promises near livestock, roads or access land, or livestock used as a scare.
- Parks, fields, beaches or council rules the research doesn't give.
- Training claims on a walking or sitting site.
- Claims about the dogs or owners in an area instead of the walking environment.

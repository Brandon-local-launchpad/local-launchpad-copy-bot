How good copy reads in this trade. Copybot loads this pack on every page type alongside the CORE prompt, the niche entry and (on service and category pages) the trade facts pack.

What lives where, so nothing is said twice:
- **CORE prompt**: the fields, word counts and every global rule (no dashes, no unconfirmed claims, no placeholders, banned phrases, how to use local research). Not repeated here.
- **Trade facts pack**: what is true of the work (materials, systems, lifespans, rules, what goes wrong). Not repeated here.
- **Niche entry**: words customers use, words to avoid, fears, step and FAQ briefs. Not repeated here.

## 1. The trade and its reader

Two different customers read a roofer's site, and the copy has to know which one it is talking to.

- **The reactive customer** has water coming through a ceiling, a gap where tiles were before the storm, or a gutter pouring down the wall. They want to know someone can come, stop the water and tell them what actually failed. Copy for them is short and steady: what happens first, why it can't wait, and what makes it safe.
- **The planned customer** is weighing a new flat roof, a replacement, roofline or a chimney. They are spending real money once, on work they will never see up close. Their fear is paying for a full roof when a repair would have done, or a repair that fails in two winters because nobody dealt with what was underneath. Copy for them names the material and the system and explains the layer they can't see.

Behind both sits the trust gap this trade is known for. Most homeowners have had, or heard of, the cold caller on the doorstep who has "noticed a few loose tiles". Honest copy answers that directly: no decision on the doorstep, look at the evidence, a second opinion is reasonable. Photographs of what was found earn more trust than any claim.

The voice is a roofer who has been up there, explaining in plain words what has failed and why. Never a salesperson, and never someone who makes a roof sound more dangerous than it is.

## 2. How this trade's services sort into the template

Code chooses the business type and the block briefs. This section tells you what each block should be *about* for each part of this trade, so the copy fits the brief.

| Category (GBP) | Type | Block 4 is about |
|---|---|---|
| Roofing services (urgent pages) | A + emergency | When to call us out: water coming in, storm damage, tiles or ridge down, a chimney or gutter that has come loose |
| Roofing services (planned pages) | A project | Common problems: symptom, cause in the material, and what it leads to if left |

One GBP category covers the trade, so the urgent versus planned split is made by page. Emergency framing belongs on the urgent pages only.

**Urgent versus planned, inside the trade.**

- **Urgent** (write situation, why it can't wait, what happens when they call): Roof repairs where the roof is leaking, Storm damage repairs, Roof window repairs where water is getting in, Chimney repairs where masonry has loosened. Never claim response times, 24 hour cover or emergency call outs unless the client confirms them.
- **Planned, problem led** (write symptom, cause, consequence): Roof repairs, Roof replacement, Flat roof replacement, Felt roofing, Slate roofing, Tile roofing, Dry ridge and dry verge, Leadwork, Chimney repairs and repointing, Gutter repairs, Fascias and soffits, Loft ventilation, Garage roof replacement, Conservatory roof replacement.
- **Planned, desire led** (write the customer's situation, never invent symptoms): Roof window installation (examples say "roof window installation"), Warm roof and cold roof conversion, Chimney removal, Guttering installation, Cladding.
- **Schedule led** (write what slips and when): Roof inspections, Gutter cleaning, Roof cleaning and moss removal, Roof coatings, Solar panel bird proofing.

EPDM rubber roofing, GRP fibreglass roofing and Commercial roofing follow the flat roof pages: problem led for replacements, decision led on the blog.

**Seasonal note.** Roofing is weather led rather than seasonal. Storms bring the urgent work in autumn and winter; leaf fall fills gutters; flat roof membranes and mortar need dry, mild days to go down properly. Where timing appears, give the reason in the material or the weather, never "winter is a busy time".

## 3. Local detail that works in this trade

Research can tell you when an area was built and what weather it gets. It cannot tell you what is on any roof today.

**Strong anchors** (area wide and checkable): housing era, written only as an era claim ("built with", "originally fitted with", "the ones not yet replaced"); wind exposure on hilltops, open edges of town and the coast; tree cover and moss on shaded slopes; salt air near the sea; tenure (landlords and managing agents with blocks of flat roofed garages or flats); storm history, only where the research gives a named storm or event; conservation areas where the research confirms them, because they change what materials a replacement can use.

**Every anchor must change the work.** A 1960s estate is only worth mentioning if it then explains the felt flat roofs on its garages and extensions and why felt fails at the laps. Wind exposure is only worth mentioning if it explains why ridge and verge fixings matter more on that side of town. Tree cover is only worth mentioning if it explains moss lifting tiles and blocked gutters.

**Do not:** claim roofs in an area are failing, old, in poor repair or "due for replacement"; describe the current state of any roof ("many homes here have sagging gutters"); invent storms, wind speeds or damage figures; name a roof covering for an area unless the research supports the era and the material together.

## 4. Craft rules for this trade

1. **Name the material and the system.** "Three layer torch on felt", "a single sheet EPDM membrane on a new deck", "concrete interlocking tiles on battens and breathable membrane", not "a new roof". The material decides how it fails and what replacing it involves.
2. **Put the failure in plain words.** Felt fails at the laps as the bitumen stiffens and cracks. Ridge mortar cracks, and the ridge tiles start to rock. Roofline brackets give and the gutter run sags, so water spills at the low point. Blisters on a flat roof are trapped moisture lifting the layers. Ponding means the fall has gone. Name the mechanism and the copy reads as diagnosis, not warning.
3. **The stain is not where the breach is.** Water travels along a flat roof deck, a batten or a rafter before it drips, so the mark on the ceiling is rarely under the fault. Saying so explains why finding a leak is a job in itself and why patching above the stain so often fails.
4. **Repair versus replace is an honest diagnosis.** One slipped tile rarely leaks at once, but the underlay beneath it degrades in the sun and rain and then it does. A sound roof with a local fault needs a repair; a roof failing across its whole area needs replacing. Copy that says both, and when each applies, is what beats the cold caller.
5. **What's underneath is the roof.** On a flat roof, the deck preparation is the job: a membrane laid over a wet or rotten deck fails whatever it is made of. On roofline, strip the old boards rather than cap over them, because capping hides the rot it sits on. Show the hidden layer.
6. **Match the register to the urgency.** Water through the ceiling and a planned replacement are different reads. Urgent copy says what happens first and what makes it safe; planned copy explains choices. Never make planned work sound urgent to sell it.

## 5. What good looks like: one example per field type

### Homepage service card (type A + emergency, Roof repairs): 40 to 55 words

```
home_services_cards:
  - card_name: Roof Repairs | card_text: A damp patch on the bedroom ceiling is usually the first sign, and the fault is rarely right above it. Our [roof repairs](/services/roof-repairs/) start by tracing where the water actually gets in, whether that's a slipped tile, cracked ridge mortar or a failed lead flashing, so the fix holds.
```

Why it works: opens on what the reader sees, corrects the belief that the leak is above the stain, and names real faults in plain words. No response time or call out claim, and no local detail (cards don't carry it).

### Category page service card (type A, Flat roof replacement): 40 to 55 words

```
cat_services_cards:
  - card_name: Flat Roof Replacement | card_text: A flat roof that holds puddles for days after rain has lost its fall, and a new membrane laid over the same deck will pond again. Our [flat roof replacement](/services/flat-roof-replacement/) work starts with the deck: rotten boards replaced and the fall corrected before the new covering goes down.
```

Why it works: the symptom leads to the hidden layer, which is the planned customer's real fear. It describes what good flat roof work involves without naming a product.

### Block 4 card, urgent (Storm damage repairs, A + emergency): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Tiles or ridge down after a storm | card_body: Once tiles or a ridge section have gone, rain reaches the underlay and battens directly, and the next gust can lift the tiles beside the gap. Making it watertight comes first, usually with a temporary cover, then the repair once it's safe to work. Photographs of the damage help with an insurance claim.
```

Why it works: situation, why it can't wait, and what happens next, written as what usually happens rather than a claim about this business's response.

### Block 4 card, problem led (Felt roofing): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Cracks along the felt laps | card_body: Felt usually fails at the laps first. As the bitumen ages it stiffens, and daily heating and cooling opens hairline cracks where one layer overlaps the next. Water finds those cracks and runs along the deck before it drips through, which is why the stain inside is so often nowhere near the split.
```

Why it works: symptom, cause in the material, and the consequence in plain words. The flat roof deck point explains why patching above the stain fails.

### Block 4 card, schedule led (Gutter cleaning): 40 to 60 words

```
cat_block4:
  - card_title: Gutters spilling over in heavy rain | card_body: Water pouring over the front of a gutter in a downpour usually means leaves and moss have settled at the outlet. Left through winter, standing water adds weight that pulls the brackets, and overflow runs down the wall, soaking brickwork and the fascia behind. Clearing them after leaf fall stops most of it.
```

Why it works: a symptom the reader sees from the window, what it leads to, and a time of year with the reason for it.

### Service page content block, paragraph 3 (Dry ridge and dry verge)

```
Ridge tiles bedded on mortar stay put while the mortar holds, but once it cracks they start to rock, and each gale works them looser. A rocking ridge lets rain into the top of the roof, where it runs down the rafters out of sight, and a tile that finally lifts in a storm can come down onto the drive or a neighbour's roof.
```

Why it works: one chain of consequence the customer would recognise, from cracked mortar to a fallen tile, with the cause named and no scare language.

### FAQ answer, trust question (Roof repairs): 50 to 70 words

```
cat_faq_items:
  - faq_question: Someone knocked and said my roof needs work. Should I worry? | faq_answer: Not until someone you chose has looked at it. Doorstep callers often point to a loose tile or ridge to start a conversation, and some push for a decision or payment on the day. Photographs of what was found and the work set out in writing let you check it for yourself, and a second opinion is always reasonable.
```

Why it works: the answer comes first in under 15 words, it speaks to the trade's trust gap honestly, and it describes what fair practice looks like without claiming it as this business's own.

### Location page content block, paragraph 1 (town)

```
Roofing in [town] follows the way the town was built. The Victorian terraces near the centre were originally fitted with slate, and on the ones not yet replaced, the nails and the lead around chimneys are what age first. The 1960s estates to the north were built with felt flat roofs on their garages and extensions, and the hill above them takes the full force of the westerly wind.
```

Why it works: era claims only ("originally fitted with", "built with", "the ones not yet replaced"), each tied to what it means for the work, plus one exposure anchor that changes the job. It says nothing about the state of any roof today.

## 6. Blog posts and cost guides

Blog posts are the longest prose on the site and the place this trade's knowledge shows most. The voice and the local detail rules above still apply, with one important difference.

**No situations on the blog.** On the website pages, urgent work is written as the customer's situation and desire led work (roof windows, warm roof conversions) as the reason someone wants it. The blog prompt bans scenarios and imagined customers, so on the blog those topics are written as *questions and decisions*: what causes a leak, what decides between repair and replacement. "Why is my flat roof ponding?" not "Imagine waking up to a drip".

**What each post type is for in this trade:**

- **Question posts**: a leak with no obvious cause, ponding and blisters, moss on tiles, a sagging gutter, condensation in the loft, whether a roof needs scaffolding. Answer with the failure in the material, then what fixes it.
- **Decision posts**: repair or replace, EPDM or GRP fibreglass or felt for a flat roof, warm roof or cold roof, dry ridge or mortar bedded ridge, slate or tile for a replacement, strip or cap over roofline, whether to remove a chimney. Say which option suits which roof, never which is "best".
- **Cost guides**: what moves the price in this trade. Access and whether scaffold is needed, the roof's size, height and pitch, the material chosen, how much of the deck or battens need replacing once the old covering is off, leadwork and chimneys, and waste removal. Explain why each one moves the price. Figures only from the PRICE rows.
- **Local and seasonal posts**: one weather or era condition in one named place: wind exposure and ridge fixings, leaf fall and gutters, why membranes need dry days.

### Blog answer (question post): 40 to 60 words

```
blog_answer: Ponding on a flat roof usually means the fall has gone. A flat roof is built with a slight slope so rain runs to the gutter, and when the deck sags or the boards beneath have softened, water collects in the dip instead. Standing water ages the covering faster and finds any weak joint.
```

Why it works: the first sentence answers on its own and makes a clean meta description. The cause is physical and the consequence is stated plainly.

### Blog section (decision post): 140 to 200 words

```
blog_sections:
- h2: Does a slipped tile mean the roof needs replacing? | body: Usually not. A single slipped or cracked tile on a roof that is otherwise sound is a repair, and it rarely lets water in straight away, because the underlay beneath is the second line of defence.\n\nThat second line doesn't last for ever once it's exposed. Sunlight and rain break down most underlays, so a gap left for months turns a small repair into a wet batten and then a damp patch inside. The sooner the tile goes back, the smaller the job stays.\n\nReplacement becomes the honest answer when the faults are spread across the whole roof rather than in one place: tiles slipping in many spots because the nails have rusted through, underlay that has turned brittle along every course, or battens that have softened. A roofer should be able to show you photographs of which of those they found. Our [roof inspections](/services/roof-inspections/) page explains what a full inspection looks at.
```

Why it works: the section opens by answering its H2, each paragraph carries one fact that would come from the trade facts rows (underlay as the second defence, underlay breaking down when exposed, what whole roof failure looks like), and it separates repair from replacement honestly. The link sits in the closing paragraph as natural text.

**What fails on the blog in this trade:** any instruction to get on a roof, use a ladder at height or patch a leak yourself, when the honest line is to stay off the roof and call a qualified roofer; national advice with no material or system named; lifespans or figures quoted from memory instead of the trade facts rows; scare posts about storms; "it depends" without naming what it depends on.

GBP descriptions, services and products don't load this pack: their prompts are built from a facts block in code.

## 7. What makes copy fail in this trade

- A sentence that could sit on any roofer's site in the country, with no material, system, fault or exposure named anywhere in the paragraph.
- Current state claims about an area ("roofs here are failing", "many homes have sagging gutters") instead of era claims.
- Fear selling: making planned work sound urgent, or a slipped tile sound like a collapse.
- "A new roof" or "roofing work" where the material and the system should be named.
- Patching language that ignores what's underneath: a membrane over a wet deck, new boards capped over old ones.
- Response times, 24 hour cover, guarantees, insurance approved status or free inspections stated as fact when not confirmed.
- Storms, wind speeds or damage events the research doesn't give.
- Emergency framing on planned pages, or planned framing on a page about water coming in.
- Brand names, including the one in the roof window page name; examples and copy say "roof window".
- Ignoring the trust gap, or answering it with claims of honesty instead of photographs, evidence and no decision on the doorstep.

## CRITICAL: THIS TRADE SERVES TWO COMPLETELY DIFFERENT MARKETS (READ FIRST)

A company offering dog training AND security dog services is selling to two audiences that have nothing in common:

**Market 1 — Dog owners (consumer).** A family whose dog pulls on the lead, barks at visitors, or ignores recall. A couple with a new puppy. Someone whose rescue dog is reactive around other dogs. These readers are emotionally invested, often embarrassed or frustrated, and they have usually already tried to fix the problem themselves. The copy is warm, plain, and reassuring without being soft. It speaks to a person about their dog.

**Market 2 — Commercial buyers (B2B).** A site manager procuring security for a construction site. A facilities manager comparing a dog unit against static guards or CCTV. An events organiser needing crowd-adjacent security. These readers are making a procurement decision. The copy is direct, capability-led, and evidence-focused. It speaks to a professional about risk, cost, and coverage.

**The two registers must NEVER blur.** Dog training copy that reads like a procurement brochure loses the family. Security dog copy with warm family language loses the site manager. Every page belongs to one market or the other — identify which before writing a word, and hold the correct register throughout.

## CRITICAL: HOW LOCAL DETAIL WORKS FOR THIS TRADE

There is no property stock angle for this trade. Never anchor local detail in housing types, property eras, or anything about buildings (except for security services, where premises types matter).

The local conditions that genuinely apply area-wide are:

**For dog training:**
- **Walking environments** — the parks, beaches, woodland, riverside paths, and open spaces where local owners actually walk their dogs. These are where recall fails, where reactivity shows, and where training has to hold.
- **Livestock proximity** — in rural and semi-rural areas, sheep and cattle fields adjoining footpaths make recall and stock avoidance a genuine safety and legal issue for local owners.
- **Urban distraction environments** — busy high streets, traffic, cyclists, other dogs at close quarters. Town-centre living demands different training priorities than village living.
- **Local demographics** — family areas produce puppy and child-and-dog household demand; areas with high rescue-dog ownership produce behaviour work; commuter areas produce separation-related demand.
- **Seasonal patterns** — puppy season, fireworks period, dark winter evening walks, summer visitor influx on coastal patches.

**For security dogs:**
- **Commercial and industrial geography** — construction activity, industrial estates, retail parks, warehousing, vacant premises. This is where demand lives.
- **Event venues and seasonal events** — showgrounds, festivals, seasonal fairs.
- **Known local patterns** — areas with plant theft, fly-tipping on vacant land, or unauthorised access issues (only where the geo research supports this — never invent crime claims about an area).

Anchor ALL local detail in these. Vary the anchor between cards — do not repeat the same park or the same industrial estate across every card.

## RULES — what every piece of copy in this trade must achieve

**Rule 1: Identify the market before writing, and hold the register**
Every page serves either dog owners or commercial buyers. Decide which from the service being written about, then hold that register through every sentence. Puppy training page: warm, plain, owner-facing. Construction site security page: direct, capability-led, buyer-facing.

**Rule 2: Use plain owner language for training problems**
Owners say pulling, barking, jumping up, not coming back, nipping, guarding food, going for other dogs. Write in those words. Trainer terminology (reactivity, resource guarding, desensitisation, counter-conditioning) belongs in the explanation of the approach, where it shows expertise — used sparingly and always in a sentence that makes its meaning obvious.

**Rule 3: Address what the owner has already tried**
Almost every training enquiry follows failed self-help: YouTube methods, a gadget lead, treats thrown at the problem, advice from the park. Copy that explains why those attempts have not held — usually consistency, timing, and the dog rehearsing the behaviour between sessions — meets the reader where they actually are.

**Rule 4: For security services, sell the capability, not the dog**
Commercial buyers are not buying a dog; they are buying deterrence, response, and coverage. Copy leads with what the unit achieves against the alternatives the buyer is comparing: static guarding, CCTV, doing nothing. Handler qualifications and licensing (only those confirmed on the onboarding form) are stated as facts, not decorated.

**Rule 5: Never guarantee behavioural outcomes**
Dogs are not machines. Never promise a fixed result, a fixed timeline to a fixed result, or that any behaviour will be eliminated. Describe what the training builds and how progress works. Where the client has confirmed a structured programme (a set number of sessions, a defined syllabus), state the structure without promising the outcome.

**Rule 6: Anchor local detail per the CRITICAL section above**
Walking environments, livestock, urban distraction, demographics, and seasonal patterns for training. Commercial geography and events for security. Never property stock. Never invented crime statistics.

**Rule 7: Omit unconfirmed facts entirely — never flag them in copy**
If a fact is not confirmed in the onboarding form (a price, a qualification, an accreditation, insurance details, session structure, licensing), the copy does not mention it. Write around what IS confirmed. Never place [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. If flagging missing information is genuinely useful, do it as a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block. The copy must always be publishable exactly as written.

**Rule 8: Never assert operational processes as fact unless confirmed**
Session length, number of sessions, whether training happens at the owner's home or a training field, group class sizes, patrol frequencies — these are only stated if the onboarding form confirms them. Otherwise describe the principle without the specifics.

**Rule 9: No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphenated compounds**
Write "well behaved" not "well-behaved." Write "on lead" not "on-lead." Write "long term" not "long-term." No exceptions.

## SECTION STRUCTURE FOR THIS TRADE (IMPORTANT — DIFFERS FROM OTHER TRADES)

The standard service page includes a signs section for problem-driven services. For this trade, that section is replaced as follows:

**Problem-driven training services** (pulling, recall, reactivity, barking, separation problems) → **"Why It Isn't Going Away On Its Own"** section. The owner already knows there is a problem. What they are weighing is whether to pay for help or wait it out. This section answers that: the dog rehearses the behaviour every day it continues, self-help fails on timing and consistency rather than effort, and the behaviour typically deepens rather than fades. Three paragraphs, three distinct reasons, each mechanism explained plainly.

**Life-stage services** (puppy training, adolescent dogs, new rescue dogs) → **"Is This Right For You"** section. Nobody needs signs that a puppy needs training. This section describes two or three owner situations the reader can recognise themselves in, and what the service gives each one.

**Security dog services** → **"When a Dog Unit Is the Right Call"** section. The buyer is comparing options, not diagnosing a problem. This section sets out the situations where a dog unit outperforms the alternatives the buyer is considering, honestly including where it is not the right fit.

The "How It Works" section remains for all services, adapted to the register of the market being served.

The seasonal tasks section is replaced for this trade: training pages use a "Training Through the Life Stages" section (puppy, adolescent, adult, senior), because demand in this trade follows the dog's age rather than the calendar. Security pages use neither a seasonal nor a life stage section; coverage timing (nights, weekends, shutdown periods) belongs in the service cards and how it works sections.

### Life stage section example (REPLACES the seasonal tasks section for this trade)

Demand in dog training is not driven by the calendar the way landscaping is. It is driven by the dog's age. The seasonal tasks section is therefore replaced by a training through the life stages section, which does the same structural job (time-organised entries that show expertise and map to services) but on the timeline that actually drives this trade: puppy, adolescent, adult, senior.

```
TRAINING THROUGH THE LIFE STAGES:

Every stage of a dog's life asks something different of its owner, and most training enquiries arrive at the transitions: the new puppy, the adolescent who has stopped listening, the adult whose habits have hardened, the older dog whose world is changing.

• Puppy, 8 weeks to around 6 months: the socialisation window closes early, and what a puppy calmly meets in its first months shapes the adult dog more than anything after. Foundations here are cheap; fixes later are not.
• Adolescence, roughly 6 to 18 months: hormones arrive and trained behaviours seem to vanish. This is normal, it is temporary, and it is the stage where most owners give up just when consistency matters most. The habits rehearsed through adolescence are the habits the adult keeps.
• Adulthood: established behaviours, good and bad, are now well rehearsed. Change is absolutely possible at this stage, but it works by building a stronger alternative habit rather than expecting the old one to fade on its own.
• Older dogs: hearing and sight changes can look like stubbornness, and pain can look like new aggression. Behaviour changes in an older dog are worth a vet conversation as well as a training one, and honest trainers say so.
```

**Why this works:**
- The section is organised on the timeline that genuinely drives demand in this trade: the dog's age, not the calendar
- Each entry contains one piece of real expertise: the early socialisation window, adolescent regression being normal and temporary, adult change working through stronger alternatives, older-dog behaviour changes warranting a vet conversation
- The older dog entry models professional honesty by pointing outside the trade (to the vet) where that is the right advice
- Nothing here asserts unconfirmed services or programme structures; it describes what each stage means, and maps naturally to whatever life stage services the client actually offers

NOTE: this section replacement needs the category page CORE prompt updated to swap the seasonal tasks section for a life stages section when the trade is dog training. ## SERVICE PAGE — WHAT GOOD LOOKS LIKE

### "Why It Isn't Going Away On Its Own" section example (reactive dog — owner market)

```
WHY IT ISN'T GOING AWAY — PARAGRAPH 1:

Every reaction is practice. When your dog barks and lunges at another dog and the other dog moves away, your dog learns that barking and lunging works. It does not matter that the other dog was leaving anyway; from your dog's side of the lead, the strategy succeeded, and behaviour that succeeds gets stronger. This is why reactive behaviour deepens over months rather than fading: the dog is not growing out of it, it is rehearsing it, and every walk where the pattern runs is another rehearsal. Waiting to see if it improves usually means watching it get more established.

WHY IT ISN'T GOING AWAY — PARAGRAPH 2:

The usual fixes fail on timing, not effort. Treats work as training tools only when they change what the dog feels before the reaction starts; handed over mid-lunge or after, they are just interruption, and sometimes they accidentally reward the exact behaviour they were meant to prevent. Crossing the road to avoid every dog keeps walks calmer, but avoidance teaches the dog nothing except that other dogs make the owner tense, which for many dogs confirms that other dogs are indeed a problem. None of this means the owner has done anything wrong. It means the problem sits in the few seconds before the reaction, and working in those seconds takes an experienced eye.

WHY IT ISN'T GOING AWAY — PARAGRAPH 3:

The world will not stop providing triggers. Around {{custom_values.biz_area_1}}, most walks mean meeting other dogs whether you plan to or not: narrow pavements, park entrances, the field where everyone walks at the same times before work. A reactive dog in a place with this much dog traffic gets triggered daily, which means daily rehearsal of the pattern. Structured training turns those same unavoidable encounters into the raw material of progress, managed at distances where your dog can cope and closing that distance only as fast as the dog succeeds.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three genuinely distinct reasons: rehearsal makes it stronger, self-help fails on timing, the environment keeps supplying triggers
- Paragraph one explains the core mechanism (behaviour that succeeds gets stronger) from the dog's side of the lead, in plain words
- Paragraph two names the two things every reactive-dog owner has tried (treats, crossing the road) and explains precisely why each fails, while explicitly removing blame
- Paragraph three uses the local anchor (dog-dense walking environment) to make the honest point that avoidance is not available, then turns the same environment into the training resource
- No outcome promised anywhere; progress is described as distance-based and success-paced

## WHAT MAKES COPY FAIL IN THIS TRADE

- Register blur: warm dog-lover language on security pages, or procurement language on training pages. Every page belongs to one market.
- Guaranteed behavioural outcomes: any promise that a behaviour will be fixed, eliminated, or resolved in a set timeframe. Living animals do not come with guarantees and honest copy says so.
- Trainer jargon used to describe the owner's problem. Owners have a dog that pulls, barks, or will not come back. Technical terms belong in explaining the approach, sparingly, in sentences that make their meaning obvious.
- Local detail anchored in property stock. This trade has no property angle for training. Anchors are walking environments, livestock, distraction settings, demographics, and seasonal patterns; commercial geography for security.
- Invented crime claims about an area. Security copy states the general pattern (losses cluster in unattended hours) without fabricating local crime statistics the geo research does not support.
- Asserted operational specifics that are not confirmed: session counts, session lengths, class sizes, home visits versus training field, patrol frequencies, handler-to-site ratios.
- Qualifications, licences, accreditations, or insurance claims not confirmed on the onboarding form. Security buyers check these; a false claim is fatal.
- [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. Unconfirmed facts are omitted entirely; genuinely valuable gaps go in a [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block.
- Seasonal task content forced onto a trade that is not seasonal. Dog training demand is life stage driven; the seasonal section is replaced by the life stages section, and security pages use neither.
- Signs sections for services where the problem is self-evident. Problem training services get "Why It Isn't Going Away On Its Own," life-stage services get "Is This Right For You," security services get "When a Dog Unit Is the Right Call."
- Em dashes, en dashes, or hyphenated compounds anywhere in the output.

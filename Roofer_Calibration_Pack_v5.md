# Roofing Company Calibration Pack (UK) — All Page Types

Use this alongside the relevant CORE copywriting prompt. This pack covers all four page types: homepage, category page, service page, and location page.

This pack contains two things: the rules that define what good copy looks like for this trade, and worked examples that show what output meeting those rules actually looks like.

## CRITICAL: HOW LOCAL DETAIL WORKS FOR ROOFING (READ FIRST)

Roofing sits between the outdoor trades and the interior trades in how local claims work, and the distinction matters:

**Legitimate area wide claims — construction era.** Houses built in an era were built with that era's roofing. "The estates built in the 1960s and 70s were built with concrete interlocking tiles and flat roofed garages" is a fact. "The UPVC roofline fitted across these streets in the 1990s improvement wave is now over thirty years old" is a fact about a generation of installations.

**Illegitimate claims — the current state of individual roofs.** Individual roofs get replaced, repaired, and maintained at different times. Never write "properties here have failing felt roofs" or "roofs in this area are at end of life." The honest framing is generational: "built with," "originally fitted with," "many are now at the age where," "the original systems that have not yet been replaced are running well past their design life." That framing is accurate for every reader: the ones with original roofs recognise themselves, and the ones who have already replaced know they are not being lied to.

**Other legitimate anchors:** wind exposure (coastal strips, elevated ground, open aspects genuinely take more weather), tree cover (moss growth and gutter loading), tenure patterns (landlord stock is maintained reactively, owner occupied stock more proactively), and verifiable storm history from the geo research.

## TRADE FRAMING (all page types)

Derive the trade framing from the client's onboarding form. Use the services they have confirmed, not a generic roofing description.

Format examples:
- General roofer: `"a UK roofing company carrying out pitched roof repairs and replacements, flat roofing, leadwork, chimney work, and guttering"`
- Flat roof and roofline specialist: `"a UK roofing company specialising in EPDM rubber flat roofing and UPVC fascia, soffit, and guttering installation"`
- Roofline specialist: `"a UK roofing company specialising in fascia, soffit, guttering, and cladding replacement"`

Never use a trade framing line that includes services or materials the client has not confirmed. A flat roofing specialist must never have slate, tile, or chimney copy written for them.

## RULES — what every piece of copy in this trade must achieve

**Rule 1: Name the material and system, not the category**
Do not write "flat roofing." Write "EPDM rubber roofing," "GRP fibreglass," or "torch on felt," whichever the client actually installs. Do not write "roof repair." Write "ridge tile rebedding," "slipped slate refixing," or "lead flashing renewal." The material is what the customer is buying and the specifics prove the roofer knows their trade.

**Rule 2: Explain the failure mode, in plain sight of the customer's experience**
Roofing customers are living with a symptom: a damp patch, a slipped tile they can see from the garden, gutters that overflow in rain. Copy names what they are seeing and explains the mechanism behind it in plain language. Felt fails at the laps as the bitumen ages and stiffens. Ridge mortar carbonates, cracks, and lets tiles rock. UPVC brackets fail and gutter runs sag. The mechanism is the expertise.

**Rule 3: One accurate technical detail per card**
A lifespan comparison with the reason behind it, an installation detail that determines whether the job lasts, or a material behaviour the customer can verify. Figures must be accurate and, where a manufacturer warranty is cited, confirmed on the onboarding form.

**Rule 4: Era claims only, never current state claims**
Per the CRITICAL section: "built with" and "many are now at the age where," never "roofs here are failing." This one framing rule separates honest local copy from generic scaremongering.

**Rule 5: Match the register to reactive or planned demand**
Active leaks and storm damage are urgent: the copy is direct, response focused, and short on ceremony. Full replacements and roofline programmes are researched purchases: the copy informs the comparison the customer is already making. Do not write urgency into a planned purchase or research prose into an emergency page.

**Rule 6: Address the trust gap head on**
Roofing has one of the worst rogue trader reputations of any UK trade, built on cold callers inventing urgent problems and work the customer can never inspect. Copy addresses this with confirmed specifics only: named accreditations, years trading, review counts, manufacturer backed guarantees, photographs of completed stages provided to the customer. Never with unverifiable reassurance.

**Rule 7: Omit unconfirmed facts entirely — never flag them in copy**
Prices, warranty periods, manufacturer names, accreditations, response times: if the onboarding form does not confirm it, the copy does not mention it. Never place [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. Genuinely valuable gaps go in a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block. The copy must always be publishable exactly as written.

**Rule 8: Never assert operational processes as fact unless confirmed**
Whether scaffolding is included in quotes, whether photos are provided at each stage, whether the team is in house: only if confirmed. Otherwise describe the principle of a properly run roofing job.

**Rule 9: No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphenated compounds**
Write "torch on felt" not "torch-on felt." Write "long term" not "long-term." Write "single ply" not "single-ply." No exceptions.

## SERVICE PAGE — WHAT GOOD LOOKS LIKE

### Signs section example (flat roof replacement)

```
SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 1:

The first sign is usually indoors: a damp patch or staining on the ceiling below the flat roof, often appearing days after rain rather than during it. Water that gets through a failed lap or a split does not drop straight through; it travels along the deck boarding until it finds a low point or a join, then shows itself somewhere that may be a metre or more from the actual breach. This travelling is why flat roof leaks get misread as condensation or plumbing, and why patch repairs applied where the stain shows so often fail: the patch and the breach are in different places. Finding the entry point, not the exit point, is the diagnosis.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 2:

The second sign is visible from a step ladder: blistering and bubbling across the felt surface. Blisters form when moisture trapped between the felt layers heats up in summer sun and the vapour pressure lifts the top layer away from the one beneath. A blistered roof may not be leaking yet, but the layers have separated where every blister stands, and the bond that made the roof waterproof is gone at each of those points. Felt in this state tends to get through the summer that caused it and fail in the autumn that follows, when rain arrives to test what the heat opened up.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 3:

The third sign is water still standing on the roof two days after rain. Flat roofs are not flat: they are built with a slight fall to move water to the outlet, and standing water means the fall has gone, through deck deflection, a blocked outlet, or a membrane that has stretched and settled into hollows. Ponding water accelerates every other ageing process the roof is undergoing, adds load the deck was not designed to hold permanently, and in winter freezes and works at the surface mechanically. A roof that ponds is not necessarily leaking yet; it is a roof operating outside its design, and the gap between those two states is where the affordable intervention lives.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three distinct signs: interior staining, surface blistering, ponding, each with its full mechanism
- The water travelling explanation in paragraph one solves a genuine mystery for the customer (why the patch over the stain failed) and demonstrates diagnostic expertise
- Paragraph two's blister mechanism is accurate (trapped moisture, vapour pressure) and ends with the honest seasonal prognosis
- Paragraph three closes on the honest framing: not necessarily leaking yet, and the affordable intervention lives in that gap

### How it works section example (EPDM installation)

```
HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 1:

The job starts with the deck, not the rubber. The existing covering is stripped back to the boarding, because what is underneath decides everything: felt that has been leaking for seasons has usually been feeding moisture into the deck, and boarding that has gone soft at the edges or around old leak points has to come out and be replaced before anything is bonded over it. Overboarding or bonding onto a compromised deck is the shortcut version of this job, and it is why some rubber roofs fail in years when the membrane itself is rated for decades. The deck inspection is where the real quote gets confirmed, and it is why a fixed price given without seeing under the old covering is a price that has guessed.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 2:

With the deck sound and dry, the membrane goes down as one piece wherever the roof allows it, sized so there are no joins in the main surface. It is bonded to the deck with adhesive across the full area, then worked flat so there are no air pockets or unbonded zones. On most garage and extension roofs a single sheet covers everything, which is the core of what makes the system durable: the field of the roof has nothing to fail at.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 3:

The edges are where EPDM jobs are won or lost. The membrane has to be terminated properly at every perimeter: dressed into gutters with a drip edge so water leaves the roof instead of tracking behind the fascia, taken up and secured at walls and upstands, and trimmed and sealed around anything that penetrates the roof. Poor edge detailing is the most common failure in amateur rubber roofing, because the field of an EPDM roof is close to indestructible and the perimeter is where the skill lives. A finished roof should shed water to the outlet with nothing standing, nothing tracking behind trims, and nothing relying on sealant alone to stay dry.
```

**Why this works:**
- Paragraph one makes deck preparation the foundation of the job and states the honest economics: a price given without seeing under the covering has guessed
- Paragraph two explains the single sheet principle in plain terms: the field has nothing to fail at
- Paragraph three names where the skill genuinely lives (edges, terminations, penetrations) and what a properly finished roof looks like, giving the customer an inspection standard they can actually use
- No unconfirmed warranty periods, manufacturer names, or process claims

# Building Company Calibration Pack (UK) — All Page Types

Use this alongside the relevant CORE copywriting prompt. This pack covers all four page types: homepage, category page, service page, and location page.

This pack contains two things: the rules that define what good copy looks like for this trade, and worked examples that show what output meeting those rules actually looks like.

---

## CRITICAL: HOW LOCAL DETAIL WORKS FOR BUILDING (READ FIRST)

Building is one of the trades where genuinely rich, verifiable local detail exists, because the things that shape a building project ARE area-wide facts:

- **Property eras are real and area-wide.** A 1960s estate genuinely is a 1960s estate. What era means for a project (hip roofs that need hip to gable conversion, solid walls versus cavity, shallow Victorian foundations) is legitimate local knowledge.
- **Planning context is the strongest anchor of all.** Which local planning authority covers the area, where conservation area boundaries run, where permitted development applies and where it does not, green belt edges. These are verifiable facts that directly shape what a homeowner can build, and they are the first thing every building customer wants to know.
- **Ground conditions matter.** Clay bearing soil affects foundation depth. This is verifiable from the geo research and genuinely shapes the work.

What must still be avoided: claims about the interiors or current condition of individual properties ("homes here have dated kitchens"), and planning claims that are not accurate for the specific local authority. A wrong permitted development distance or a wrong council name destroys credibility instantly with a customer who has been researching for months.

## TRADE FRAMING (all page types)

Derive the trade framing from the client's onboarding form. Use the services they have confirmed, not a generic building description.

Format examples:
- General builder: `"a UK building company carrying out extensions, loft conversions, kitchen and bathroom installations, and property refurbishments"`
- Extension specialist: `"a UK building company specialising in single and double storey extensions, garage conversions, and structural alterations"`
- Interior specialist: `"a UK building company carrying out kitchen installations, bathroom renovations, carpentry, and internal refurbishment"`

Never use a trade framing line that includes services the client has not confirmed.

## RULES — what every piece of copy in this trade must achieve

**Rule 1: Name the project type and structural reality, not the category**
Do not write "we do extensions." Write "single storey rear extensions with a steel beam spanning the knockout into the existing kitchen" or "hip to gable loft conversions with a rear dormer." The specifics tell a homeowner this builder has done their exact project before.

**Rule 2: Planning information must be locally accurate or absent**
Planning is the first question every building customer asks, and most have already researched it. Standard permitted development allows rear single storey extensions up to 3 metres on semi detached and terraced houses and 4 metres on detached, within height and eaves limits, with larger extensions possible through the prior approval neighbour consultation route. Conservation areas and Article 4 directions remove these rights. The correct local planning authority name, the conservation area reality for the specific area, and accurate limits come from the geo research. If the geo research does not confirm the local planning position, state the general rule and recommend the site visit rather than guessing local specifics.

**Rule 3: One structural or project detail per card that a homeowner can follow**
A foundation depth with the soil reason. Why a structural engineer specifies the beam. Why the knockout happens last. The detail must be accurate and must not assert this client's specific process unless the onboarding form confirms it.

**Rule 4: Classify every service as desire driven or problem driven, and use the right section**
Extensions, loft conversions, kitchens, bathrooms, and garden rooms are desire driven: nobody needs signs they want a bigger kitchen. These services get an "Is This Right For You" section describing owner situations. Structural repairs, subsidence work, damp work, and renovation of defects are problem driven: these get a signs section that explains what the homeowner is seeing and what it means.

**Rule 5: Respect the long research cycle**
Building customers research for months before contacting anyone. Copy wins by being genuinely useful to that research: accurate planning context, realistic sequencing, honest complexity. Marketing claims lose to information in this trade.

**Rule 6: Address the specific fears of this trade**
The fears are concrete: hidden costs appearing mid project, the job overrunning, the builder disappearing, cowboy work failing inspection. Copy addresses these with specifics that are confirmed on the onboarding form (fixed written quotes, staged payments, Building Regulations sign off included) and never with "trustworthy and reliable" filler.

**Rule 7: Omit unconfirmed facts entirely — never flag them in copy**
Prices, timelines, guarantee terms, accreditations, insurance details, staged payment structures: if the onboarding form does not confirm it, the copy does not mention it. Never place [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. Genuinely valuable gaps go in a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block. The copy must always be publishable exactly as written.

**Rule 8: Never assert operational processes as fact unless confirmed**
Whether this client handles planning applications, uses an in house architectural technician, offers fixed quotes, or works in staged payments is only stated if confirmed. Otherwise describe the principle of a well run project without claiming the specifics.

**Rule 9: No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphenated compounds**
Write "single storey" not "single-storey." Write "hip to gable" not "hip-to-gable." Write "long term" not "long-term." No exceptions.

### Signs section example (structural repairs — problem driven)

```
SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 1:

The clearest sign is a crack that comes back. Hairline cracks in plaster are normal in any house and redecorating deals with them permanently. A crack that reopens along the same line within months of being filled is different: the building is still moving, and the crack is the movement showing through. The pattern matters as much as the size. Diagonal cracks stepping through brickwork above doors and windows, wider at the top than the bottom, point toward foundation movement rather than settled plaster. On the clay ground under much of {{custom_values.biz_area_1}}, some seasonal movement is normal, and the distinction between that and progressive movement is exactly what a structural assessment establishes.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 2:

The second sign is doors and windows that have started catching where they used to close cleanly. Frames are rigid rectangles fixed into the structure, so when the structure moves even slightly, the frames go out of square before anything else shows. A door that suddenly needs a shoulder in a house that never had that problem is often the earliest visible evidence of movement, appearing well before cracking does. Timber swelling in damp winters causes the same symptom and means nothing, which is why the useful question is whether the catching follows the seasons or has arrived and stayed.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 3:

The third sign is damp appearing on a wall with no plumbing anywhere near it. In the solid brick walls of {{custom_values.biz_area_1}}'s Victorian and Edwardian stock, moisture can track from outside straight through the wall where pointing has eroded, render has cracked, or outside ground levels have been built up above the damp course by decades of path and patio work. This is regularly misdiagnosed as rising damp and treated with an injected course that cannot fix it, because the water is coming through the wall, not up it. Finding the actual path the moisture takes is the repair; everything else is redecorating over a returning problem.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three distinct signs with three distinct mechanisms: recurring cracks, frames out of square, moisture pathways
- Each paragraph separates the harmless version from the meaningful version (settled plaster versus movement, seasonal timber swelling versus arrived and stayed, rising damp versus penetrating), which is honest and prevents scaremongering
- The misdiagnosis point in paragraph three (injected courses that cannot fix penetrating damp) is genuine expertise that protects the customer from the wrong purchase
- Local anchors are era and soil, both legitimate for this trade

### How it works section example (rear extension)

```
HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 1:

Every project starts with the site visit, because no two extensions price the same even on identical house types. The visit establishes the planning position for your specific property, what the ground conditions mean for foundations, where the drains run, and whether the party wall with next door comes into play. These four things set the real scope of the project, and skipping any of them at the start is how mid project surprises happen. Quoting before establishing them is guessing, and a guess in your favour at quote stage becomes a cost against you at build stage.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 2:

Design and approvals come next, and there are two separate approvals that homeowners often expect to be one. Planning permission, where it is needed at all, is about whether the extension can exist. Building Regulations approval is always needed and is about whether it is built properly: foundation depth, structural steel sized by an engineer, insulation values, drainage. The drawings produced at this stage serve both, and they are also the documents the build is priced from, which is what makes a fixed scope possible.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 3:

The build itself runs from the ground up in a sequence that does not change: foundations dug and poured to the depth the ground requires, which on clay in {{custom_values.biz_area_1}} typically means deeper excavation than on free draining ground; then walls to damp course, the structure and roof, and making it watertight. The knockout into the existing house comes last, after the new structure is sealed, because the shorter the period your kitchen wall is open to the weather, the better the project is being run. That single sequencing decision is one of the clearest differences between an experienced builder and a cheap one.
```

**Why this works:**
- Paragraph one names the four things that set real scope and states the honest economics of guessed quotes
- Paragraph two untangles the planning versus Building Regulations confusion, which is the most common misunderstanding in the trade
- Paragraph three connects foundation depth to local soil, and the knockout last principle is real construction sequencing knowledge with the reason attached
- Nothing asserts this specific client's process details beyond principles of a well run project

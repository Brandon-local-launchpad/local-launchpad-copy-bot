## CRITICAL: HOW LOCAL DETAIL WORKS FOR DRAINAGE (READ FIRST)

Drainage has unusually strong local anchors, because what is buried under a property genuinely does follow area wide patterns:

- **Property era predicts pipework.** Unlike carpets or kitchens, drains are rarely wholly replaced: a Victorian terrace almost always still has its original clay pipework and shared drain layout, whatever has been done inside the house. Era claims about what is underground are legitimate in this trade.
- **Soil is real.** Clay ground moves seasonally and cracks and displaces rigid pipework; free draining ground washes out around leaking joints; high water tables push groundwater into damaged drains. This comes from the geo research and applies area wide.
- **Trees are real.** Tree lined streets generate root ingress into jointed pipework, consistently and verifiably.
- **The water company boundary is a fact.** Since 2011, homeowners own the drains inside their boundary while lateral drains and sewers beyond it belong to the water company. Naming the correct local water company and explaining this split is some of the most useful copy this trade can carry, because it is the single most common point of customer confusion.

What must still be avoided: invented flood events or crime style scare claims, and asserting the current condition of any individual property's drains. The honest framing mirrors roofing: "the original clay pipework that most of these streets still run on," not "your drains are failing."

**The two registers of this trade are extreme and must not blur.** Emergency customers have sewage backing up right now: they need speed, a number, and a clear price, in that order, and they are reading on a phone in distress. Planned customers (homebuyer surveys, landlords, relining decisions) are comparing quotes calmly. Every page belongs clearly to one register.

## TRADE FRAMING (all page types)

Derive the trade framing from the client's onboarding form. Use the services they have confirmed, not a generic description.

Format examples:
- Full service: `"a UK drainage company carrying out emergency drain unblocking, CCTV drain surveys, drain repairs, relining, and excavation"`
- Survey led: `"a UK drainage company specialising in CCTV drain surveys, homebuyer drainage reports, and drain repairs"`
- With septic work: `"a UK drainage company carrying out drain unblocking, CCTV surveys, drain repairs, and septic tank and soakaway services"`

Never use a trade framing line that includes services the client has not confirmed.

## RULES — what every piece of copy in this trade must achieve

**Rule 1: Name the method, not the category**
Do not write "drain cleaning." Write "high pressure water jetting," "mechanical root cutting," "CCTV survey," "no dig relining," or "excavation and repair," whichever the client actually offers. The method is what determines whether the problem stays fixed, and customers who have had a blockage return after a cheap rodding job know the difference.

**Rule 2: Evidence is the trust currency of this trade**
Customers cannot see underground pipework, and the fear of being sold relining or excavation they do not need is the defining anxiety of this market. Copy leans on evidence at every opportunity: diagnosis by camera, footage shown to the customer, recommendations based on what the camera found rather than what the worst case would pay. Never oversell; the honest register wins here more than in any other trade.

**Rule 3: Explain the mechanism behind the symptom**
A gurgling plughole is air being pulled through a partial blockage. A recurring blockage in the same spot is a defect (a displaced joint, root mass, or a belly in the pipe) that catches everything that passes, not bad luck. A drain smell indoors is often a dried out trap or a failed seal, not always a blocked drain. The mechanism explained plainly is what separates expert copy from a list of services.

**Rule 4: Get the ownership boundary right**
Homeowners own the drains within their property boundary. Lateral drains beyond the boundary and shared sewers transferred to the water company in 2011. This means some problems are the water company's to fix at no cost to the homeowner, and honest copy says so, names the local water company from the geo research, and explains when to call them first. Telling a customer when they do not need to pay you is the strongest trust move available in this trade.

**Rule 5: Match the register to the emergency or planned demand**
Emergency pages and cards: short sentences, speed and clarity first, phone number early, no ceremony. Planned pages (surveys, relining, root programmes): informative comparison copy for a calm reader. Never blur them.

**Rule 6: Era and ground claims yes, individual condition claims no**
"The Victorian terraces along these roads still run on their original clay pipework and shared drain runs" is legitimate. "Your drains are failing" is not. Mirror the roofing framing: original systems, still in place, at the age where.

**Rule 7: Omit unconfirmed facts entirely — never flag them in copy**
Callout fees, response times, prices, guarantees, accreditations: if the onboarding form does not confirm it, the copy does not mention it. Never place [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. Genuinely valuable gaps go in a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block. The copy must always be publishable exactly as written.

**Rule 8: Never assert operational processes as fact unless confirmed**
Whether the client shows footage on the day, provides recorded surveys, offers fixed callout pricing, or attends same day: only if confirmed. Otherwise describe the principle of evidence led drainage work without claiming the specifics.

**Rule 9: No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphenated compounds**
Write "no dig relining" not "no-dig relining." Write "high pressure jetting" not "high-pressure jetting." Write "long term" not "long-term." No exceptions.

## SERVICE PAGE — WHAT GOOD LOOKS LIKE

### Signs section example (drain repair)

```
SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 1:

The clearest sign is the blockage that keeps coming back in the same place. A one off blockage is usually just what went down the drain; a repeating one is the pipe itself. Displaced joints leave an edge standing in the flow that catches paper and waste, root masses regrow from the joint they entered through, and a section that has lost its fall becomes a low spot where solids settle out of slow moving water. Each clearance buys weeks or months, then the same catch point rebuilds. The interval tells you something too: regrowth from roots tends to come back on a season's rhythm, while a displaced joint catches at random depending on what is flushed.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 2:

The second sign is gurgling from plugholes and toilets, especially when something else drains. That sound is air being pulled through water in the traps, and it means the air in the pipe is not moving freely: somewhere downstream, a partial blockage or a defect is restricting the bore, and the system is breathing through your plugholes instead. Gurgling on its own rarely feels urgent, which is exactly its value: it is the early warning that arrives weeks before the water stops going down, while the fix is still a clean rather than an emergency.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 3:

The third sign is the one outside: a patch of lawn that stays lush and green in a dry spell, ground that has settled or sunk along a line, or a smell near a particular spot in the garden. A cracked or open jointed drain leaks nutrient rich water into the surrounding ground, and the grass above it thrives while the pipe fails. On clay ground, the leak also softens and moves the soil around the pipe, which is how a small defect becomes a displaced run. The garden is often the most honest witness a drain has; it just reports slowly.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three distinct signs with three distinct mechanisms: the recurring catch point, the breathing system, the garden's evidence
- Paragraph one adds genuine diagnostic depth: the return interval itself carries information
- Paragraph two reframes the non urgent symptom as the valuable one: the early warning while the fix is still cheap
- Paragraph three's lush grass mechanism is accurate and memorable, and the clay interaction connects to the local ground condition

### How it works section example (CCTV survey and repair)

```
HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 1:

The camera only tells the truth in a clean pipe, so most surveys start with jetting. A camera pushed through a dirty drain shows deposits and standing water and not much else; the same camera through a scoured pipe shows the walls, the joints, and the faults exactly as they are. The survey then runs the full length of the drain, recording as it goes, and noting the position and depth of anything it finds, which is what turns a fault on a screen into a fault that can be repaired at a precise point rather than guessed at.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 2:

What the camera finds decides what happens next, and it is a shorter list than the trade sometimes pretends: nothing wrong beyond the blockage that was cleared, in which case the survey is the whole job; wear and minor defects worth monitoring but not repairing, in which case honesty costs a sale and earns a customer; damage that suits a liner, cured in place without excavation; or damage past lining, where a targeted dig at the recorded position replaces the failed section and nothing else. The footage is what stands behind whichever answer comes back. You should expect to see it, not be told about it.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 3:

Ownership gets checked before anything is quoted, because not every fault under your garden is yours to pay for. Drains inside your boundary are yours; lateral drains beyond it and shared runs serving more than one property have belonged to the water company since 2011, and faults on their side are theirs to repair at no cost to you. On the older shared systems common across {{custom_values.biz_area_1}}, this matters constantly, and a survey that finds the fault three metres past your boundary has just saved you the cost of someone else's repair. An honest drainage company tells you when the call you need to make is to the water company, not to them.
```

**Why this works:**
- Paragraph one explains why jetting precedes the camera, which most customers do not know and which makes the combined visit make sense
- Paragraph two lays out all four honest outcomes including the two that earn the company nothing, and "expect to see it, not be told about it" hands the customer the standard to hold any company to
- Paragraph three deploys the ownership boundary as the trade's biggest trust move: telling the customer when not to pay you
- No response times, prices, or unconfirmed process claims anywhere

### Local knowledge section example

```
LOCAL KNOWLEDGE — PARAGRAPH 1:

The Victorian and Edwardian streets around the centre of {{custom_values.biz_area_2}} still run on the drainage they were built with: jointed clay pipework and shared runs that cross several properties before they reach the sewer. The shared layouts matter twice over. LOCAL KNOWLEDGE — PARAGRAPH 2:

The clay ground under most of {{custom_values.biz_area_2}} adds its own signature. Clay swells wet and shrinks dry, and a century of those cycles works the joints of rigid pipework loose a fraction at a time: a displaced edge here, an opened joint there, each one a catch point for waste and a doorway for roots. The dry summers show it most; the ground movement of a long hot spell is often followed by an autumn of drains that were fine in July misbehaving by October. It is also why the town's tree lined roads produce such consistent root work, because every opened joint under a mature tree is an invitation.

LOCAL KNOWLEDGE — PARAGRAPH 3:

The newer estates on the edge of {{custom_values.biz_area_2}} run on plastic pipework with sealed joints, which roots cannot enter and clay movement flexes rather than cracks, so the era problems of the centre largely do not apply. What these estates generate instead is surface water work: soakaways dug into clay that never drained well, gullies that overload in heavy rain, and the occasional adopted pumping station drama on the developments that have one. Different stock, different faults, and a useful reminder that drainage problems follow what is underground, not the age of the kitchen above it.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three genuinely distinct zones with three distinct fault patterns: shared Victorian runs, clay worked joints and roots, modern plastic with surface water problems
- Paragraph one deploys the ownership point where it is most valuable, on the shared stock
- Paragraph two connects the dry summer to the autumn misbehaviour with the actual mechanism, and makes the tree work make sense
- Paragraph three is honest about where the era problems do not apply, which makes every other claim more credible

# Cleaning Company Calibration Pack (UK) — All Page Types

Use this alongside the relevant CORE copywriting prompt. This pack covers all four page types: homepage, category page, service page, and location page.

This pack contains two things: the rules that define what good copy looks like for this trade, and worked examples that show what output meeting those rules actually looks like.

The examples use `{{custom_values.X}}` placeholders exactly as the bot outputs them. The local detail in the examples is illustrative. When writing for an actual client, the same format and depth applies but all local detail must come from the geo research for that client's area.

Do not copy the examples. Use them to understand the required length, structure, and level of specificity, then write to the same standard for the actual client.

---

## CRITICAL: HOW LOCAL DETAIL WORKS FOR CLEANING (READ FIRST)

Cleaning is not like outdoor trades. A landscaper can say "gardens in this area sit on clay soil" because soil type applies to every garden in the area. A cleaning company CANNOT say "homes in this area have wool carpets" or "properties here have older flooring" — because what is inside people's homes varies house by house. One property has new laminate, the next has 20 year old carpet, the next has tile. Any claim about what is inside homes across an area is a guess and reads as generic filler to anyone whose home does not match.

The local conditions that genuinely apply area-wide for a cleaning company are:

- **Tenure patterns** — where rental stock is concentrated, where owner-occupiers dominate, where HMOs and student lets cluster. This is verifiable and drives demand type.
- **Letting agent behaviour** — checkout standards, receipt requirements, turnaround expectations in this specific market
- **Demographics** — family areas, student areas, retirement areas, commuter populations. These drive service type and buying behaviour.
- **Commercial premises mix** — offices, schools, care homes, hospitality venues that generate contract and one-off commercial demand
- **Seasonal demand cycles** — academic year tenancy handovers, pre-Christmas domestic bookings, post-summer commercial returns
- **Local events and situations** — a large employer, a university, a holiday let market, a new housing development

Anchor ALL local detail in these. Never anchor it in claims about carpet types, floor ages, or interior conditions across an area.

## TRADE FRAMING (all page types)

Derive the trade framing from the client's onboarding form. Use the services they have confirmed, not a generic cleaning description.

Format: `"a UK cleaning company carrying out [CLIENT'S CONFIRMED SERVICES]"`

Examples of correct trade framing by specialism:
- Carpet cleaning specialist: `"a UK carpet and upholstery cleaning company using hot water extraction and low moisture cleaning systems"`
- End of tenancy operator: `"a UK cleaning company specialising in end of tenancy cleans, move-out cleaning, and landlord compliance cleaning"`
- Commercial cleaning operator: `"a UK commercial cleaning company carrying out office cleaning, school cleaning, and contract janitorial work"`
- Broader domestic operator: `"a UK domestic cleaning company carrying out carpet cleaning, upholstery cleaning, end of tenancy cleaning, and oven cleaning"`

Never use a trade framing line that includes services the client has not confirmed on their onboarding form.

## RULES — what every piece of copy in this trade must achieve

**Rule 1: Name the specific method, not the service category**
Do not write "carpet cleaning" when describing what happens. Write "hot water extraction" or "low moisture cleaning." The method determines the result, the drying time, and the price. Always use the method the client confirmed on their onboarding form.

**Rule 2: Use plain customer language for the problem**
Customers say stains, smells, dirt, grime, marks, and odour. Write in those words. Do not use trade jargon for the problem itself. Trade terminology belongs in the explanation of the solution (enzymatic treatment, extraction, agitation) where it demonstrates expertise — not in describing what the customer sees.

**Rule 3: Explain why the customer's own attempts have not worked**
The customer has almost always tried to fix the problem before calling. Supermarket stain remover on the carpet. Oven cleaner from the shelf. A rented machine. Copy that explains specifically why those attempts fail — and what a professional method does differently — converts better than copy that just lists what the service includes.

**Rule 4: Anchor local detail in tenure, demographics, and demand patterns**
Never in claims about interiors. See the CRITICAL section above. Every page needs local detail, and it must come from what is genuinely shared across the area: who lives there, who owns what, what the letting agents require, what businesses operate there, and when demand spikes.

**Rule 5: Vary the local anchor between service cards**
Do not use the same local angle on every card. One card can anchor in the rental market, another in family demographics, another in commercial premises, another in a seasonal cycle. Repeating one local fact across every card reads as template filler.

**Rule 6: Match the register to the demand type**
Emergency and urgent services (flood extraction, same-day tenancy cleans, accident cleanup) get direct, action-focused copy. Planned services (regular domestic cleans, commercial contracts, pre-event deep cleans) get research-friendly copy that helps a comparison shopper decide. Do not mix the registers.

**Rule 7: Address the in-home trust barrier**
A cleaning company sends a person into the customer's home, often unaccompanied. Trust signals must be specific and confirmed on the onboarding form: insurance, DBS checks if held, named operative consistency, years trading, review counts. Never claim a trust signal not confirmed.

**Rule 8: No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphenated compounds**
Write "hot water extraction" not "hot-water extraction." Write "same day" not "same-day." Write "end of tenancy" not "end-of-tenancy." No exceptions.

**Rule 9: Omit unconfirmed facts entirely — never flag them in copy**
If a fact is not confirmed in the onboarding form (a price, a guarantee duration, warranty terms, insurance details, an accreditation), the copy does not mention it at all. Write the answer around what IS confirmed and stop there. Never place [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. If it is useful to flag missing information, do it as a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block, formatted like the VA notes. The copy itself must always be complete and publishable exactly as written.

**Rule 10: Never assert operational processes as fact unless the onboarding form confirms them**
Cleaning sequences, time allocations per task, which room is done first, how long an oven takes — these vary by company and by property. Do not present a specific sequence or duration as this company's fixed process unless the client has confirmed it. Describe the principle instead: a planned working order, no cross-contamination between spaces, thoroughness on the items that fail checkouts. The principle is always true; the specifics are only true if confirmed.

## SERVICE PAGE — WHAT GOOD LOOKS LIKE

### Signs section example (pet stain and odour removal)

```
SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 1:

The clearest sign is a smell that comes back within a day or two of cleaning. Surface cleaning removes what is sitting in the carpet fibres, but pet urine does not stay in the fibres. It soaks through to the backing and the underlay within minutes of the accident, and once it dries there, the crystallised residue releases odour every time the room warms up. A carpet can be shampooed, smell fresh for forty-eight hours, and then return to exactly how it was. That cycle repeating is not bad luck or a poor clean. It means the source of the smell has never been treated, and no amount of surface cleaning will change that.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 2:

The second sign is a stain that keeps reappearing in the same spot. This happens through a process called wicking: residue deep in the carpet backing travels back up to the surface as the carpet dries after each clean, bringing the stain with it. The stain looks removed while the carpet is damp, then gradually returns over the following days. Homeowners often go through this cycle three or four times with shop-bought products before calling a professional, each attempt pushing more product residue into the carpet, which itself attracts dirt and makes the patch resoil faster than the carpet around it.

SIGNS SECTION — PARAGRAPH 3:

The third sign is a smell that gets worse after cleaning rather than better. This is the most reliable indicator that the contamination is deep. Water from the cleaning process reaches the dried residue in the backing and underlay and reactivates it, so the room smells stronger as the carpet dries than it did before the clean started. People who experience this usually conclude the cleaner made things worse. What actually happened is that the clean confirmed how deep the problem goes. At this point an enzymatic treatment of the source is the only approach that will hold, and in the heaviest cases the affected section of underlay needs replacing before the carpet above it can be saved.
```

**Why this works:**
- Three genuinely distinct signs: returning smell, returning stain, smell worsening after cleaning
- Each has its mechanism explained in plain language: soaking through to underlay, wicking, water reactivating residue
- Paragraph two includes the detail about product residue attracting dirt — accurate, and explains a secondary problem the customer has probably noticed
- Paragraph three addresses the customer's likely conclusion (the cleaner made it worse) and reframes it honestly
- No local property claims anywhere — the mechanism is universal, and that is fine for a signs section
- Each paragraph is 110-130 words

### How it works section example (end of tenancy cleaning)

```
HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 1:

Every end of tenancy clean starts with the inventory. The checkout report is compared against the check-in condition, and the gap between the two is what a landlord can charge against a deposit. Letting agents in {{custom_values.biz_area_1}} work from standard checkout clauses: carpets professionally cleaned where the check-in report says they were, appliances returned to their recorded condition, and written evidence of professional cleaning in the form of a receipted invoice that names the tasks completed. A receipt that just says "cleaning" is routinely challenged. {{custom_values.company_name}} provides an itemised receipt naming every task, every room, and the date, which is the format agents accept without question.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 2:

The clean works through the property in a planned order rather than room by room at random, and the order exists for a reason: wet room products stay in wet rooms, nothing gets dropped onto a surface that has already been finished, and carpets are done once every job that could land anything on them is complete. The exact sequence is planned around the property itself, because a flat with one bathroom and a house with three are different jobs. What stays constant is the principle: no cross-contamination between spaces, no finished surface touched twice, and nothing missed because it was assumed someone else had done it. It is a small logistical discipline that makes the difference between a property that passes checkout first time and one that needs a return visit.

HOW IT WORKS — PARAGRAPH 3:

The oven is one of the most commonly failed items at checkout, and how long it takes depends entirely on its condition: an oven that has been maintained through the tenancy is a quick job, while one that has gone unopened for years of cooking can be the longest single item in the property. Baked-on carbon builds up on the fan housing, the back panel, and the inside of the glass, and shelf-bought oven products do not shift it once it has set. Professional treatment lifts those deposits from the cavity and the removable parts, and the finished oven photographs clean, which matters, because checkout assessments are made from the photographs.
```

**Why this works:**
- Paragraph one anchors in the real commercial mechanics: inventory, checkout clauses, receipt format — locally grounded through letting agent behaviour, not interior claims
- Paragraph two describes the working principle (no cross-contamination, no finished surface touched twice, carpets last) without asserting a fixed room order as fact — the sequence is planned around the property, which is honest because every property is different
- The closing line of paragraph two connects the discipline to the outcome the customer cares about: passing checkout first time
- Paragraph three ties the oven's duration to its condition rather than claiming it always takes the most time — an oven maintained through a tenancy is a quick job, one neglected for years is not, and the copy says so
- The photograph detail in paragraph three is insider knowledge that builds trust: checkout assessments are made from the photographs
- Each paragraph is 110-140 words

## WHAT MAKES COPY FAIL IN THIS TRADE

- Claims about what is inside homes across an area: carpet types, floor ages, interior conditions. These vary house by house and read as filler. Local detail comes from tenure, demographics, letting agents, commercial mix, and demand timing.
- Trade jargon used to describe the customer's problem. Customers have stains, smells, and dirt — not "soiling." Trade terms belong in the explanation of the method, not the description of the problem.
- The same local angle repeated across every service card. Vary the anchor: rental market on one card, family demographics on another, commercial premises on another.
- Cleaning method or chemistry claims inconsistent with what the client confirmed on their onboarding form.
- Drying time figures that do not match the client's actual equipment.
- Trust claims not confirmed on the onboarding form. Never claim DBS checks, insurance, or accreditations not confirmed.
- Price ranges without confirmed figures — omit if not confirmed; flag as [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] below the copy block if valuable.
- Signs paragraphs that describe the same symptom three ways instead of three distinct mechanisms.
- FAQ answers that say "it depends" without specifying what it depends on.
- Services not confirmed on the onboarding form, and copy implying commercial capability for a domestic-only operator or vice versa.
- [CLIENT TO CONFIRM] or any bracketed placeholder inside copy. Unconfirmed facts are omitted entirely, and any flag for missing information goes in a separate [NOTE FOR BRANDON: ...] line below the copy block.
- Operational processes asserted as fact without confirmation: fixed cleaning sequences, time claims per task, or "this always takes the longest" statements. Describe principles; only state specifics the client has confirmed.
- Em dashes, en dashes, or hyphenated compounds anywhere in the output.
